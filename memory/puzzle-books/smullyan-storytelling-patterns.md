## Core Storytelling Frameworks

### 1. The Door/Room Choice (Lady or Tiger)
- **Setup:** King tests prisoners with doors hiding ladies or tigers
- **Signs on doors** provide logical clues
- **Rule systems** change between trial sets (one true/one false → both true or both false → sign truth linked to contents)
- **Why it works:** Life-or-death stakes make abstract logic urgent
- **K-6 adaptation:** Replace tiger with silly consequence (broccoli room, tickle monster). Replace lady with treasure/pet. Use 2 doors for G1-3, 3 doors for G4-6.

### 2. The Knight/Knave Island
- **Setup:** Island where knights always tell truth, knaves always lie
- **Progressive extensions:** Normals (sometimes lie), Spies (strategic), Day-dependent lying, Werewolves (hidden property), Human/Zombie + Bal/Da language barrier, Sane/Insane × Human/Vampire
- **The Fundamental Principle:** Ask "Is P equivalent to the statement that you are a knight?" — both types give same answer
- **K-6 adaptation:** "Truthville" where some animals always tell truth, others always fib. Start with 2 characters, build to 3.

### 3. The Enchanted Forest (Mockingbird)
- **Setup:** Forest where birds respond to names by calling other names (function application as bird behavior)
- **Anthropomorphized math:** Birds are "fond of" each other (fixed points), "egocentric" (self-referential), "aristocratic" or "common"
- **K-6 adaptation:** "Echo Bird" (identity), "Flip Bird" (swap), "Copy Bird" (doubling). Trace chains of responses.

### 4. The Casket/Treasure Choice (Portia's Caskets / Bellini-Cellini)
- **Setup:** Gold, silver, lead caskets with inscriptions. One contains treasure.
- **Rule:** At most one inscription true / exactly one true / maker determines truth
- **Visual:** Boxes with clue text — natural UI element for mobile app
- **K-6 adaptation:** Three treasure chests with clue cards. Directly gamifiable.

### 5. The Courtroom Drama
- **Setup:** King's court, trial of suspects, each makes a statement
- **Progression:** 3 suspects → complex chains → meta-deduction (what the judge could deduce)
- **K-6 adaptation:** "Detective Academy" — solve mysteries from witness statements

### 6. The 1001 Nights Frame (Scheherazade)
- **Setup:** Scheherazade presents puzzles to save her life
- **Stakes create engagement** — puzzle quality = survival
- **Natural escalation** — King demands harder puzzles as he gets better
- **K-6 adaptation:** Daily puzzle character who must present a worthy challenge each day (maps directly to daily puzzle system!)

### 7. The Forgetful Narrator (White Knight)
- **Setup:** Character who creates puzzles through his own forgetfulness — can't remember names, statements, or facts
- **Transforms "given info" puzzles into "salvage what you can" puzzles**
- **K-6 adaptation:** "Professor Banyan forgot the numbers!" — students piece together partial information

## 10 Narrative Techniques

### 1. Socratic Dialogue
Character poses question → Student guesses (usually wrong) → Character corrects → Student asks "why?" → Explanation follows. Reader learns alongside the student character.

### 2. The Common Wrong Answer
Design puzzles where the obvious first-guess is wrong. Name the wrong answer explicitly, then show why it fails.
- Frog in well: NOT 30 days (answer: 28)
- Clock strikes 12: NOT 60 seconds (answer: 66)
- Art dealer sells two paintings: NOT break-even (answer: $20 loss)

### 3. Escalating Difficulty Within a Theme
Each chapter: trivially easy → moderate → tricky → meta-level. Change ONE variable at a time. Let reader build mastery before adding complexity.

### 4. The "Impossible Puzzle" Technique
Occasionally present puzzles that CANNOT be solved. Recognizing impossibility is itself valid mathematical reasoning. Creates surprise and teaches critical thinking.

### 5. The Trick Question / Wordplay
Misdirection puzzles that teach careful reading: "A million divided by a quarter" (answer: 4 million, not 250,000). "Eleven thousand eleven hundred and eleven" (answer: 12,111).

### 6. The Boastful Teacher (Humpty Dumpty)
Know-it-all character who delights in student's confusion, says "that's the beautiful part!" — boastfulness is entertaining and keeps engagement.

### 7. Working Backwards
Give the end state, ask for the beginning. (Four friends take half+1 of candies; last gets 1. How many originally?) Teaching reversal thinking.

### 8. The "Tell a Friend" Test
If a kid wouldn't describe this puzzle to a friend at lunch, it's not interesting enough. Best puzzles are ones kids WANT to share.

### 9. Frame-Within-Frame Narration
Author tells children a story about Alice who encounters characters who tell her puzzles. Three narrative layers create a "being told a story" feel.

### 10. The Grand Reveal
Build confusion, then deliver a clean insight that resolves everything. The "aha!" is the reward.

## Difficulty Escalation Model (6 Levels)

| Level | Description | Smullyan Source | Grade |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|-------|
| L1 | Single binary decision | 1 character, 1 statement | G1-2 |
| L2 | Two-character interaction | 2 characters with linked statements | G2-3 |
| L3 | Three-character web | 3 suspects, interlocking claims | G3-4 |
| L4 | Multi-character chain | 4+ characters with cascading testimony | G4-5 |
| L5 | Meta-deduction | Reasoning about what ANOTHER solver could deduce | G5-6 |
| L6 | Nested meta-deduction | Reasoning about reasoning about reasoning | Olympiad |

## Key Puzzle Categories for Kiwimath Adaptation

### Tier 1: Directly Usable (Ages 6-8)
- Trick questions / common-wrong-answer traps
- Simple "who is telling truth?" with 2 characters
- Working-backwards candy/tart problems
- "Portions" method for ratio problems (no algebra needed)
- Frog-in-well type sequential puzzles

### Tier 2: Adaptable (Ages 8-10)
- Door choice puzzles (2-3 doors with sign logic)
- Knight/Knave simplified as "Truth-teller/Fibber"
- Casket/treasure box selection
- Detective/courtroom deduction from statements
- Day-dependent logic (character lies on certain days)

### Tier 3: Advanced (Ages 10-12)
- Three-way classification (knight/knave/spy → truth-teller/fibber/wildcard)
- Conditional logic ("If I am a truth-teller, then...")
- Single-question determination (find ONE question that works)
- Metapuzzles (knowing puzzle is solvable is itself a clue)
- The "convince the guard" challenge (what statement works?)

## The Golden Formula: Story First, Math Second
Every Smullyan puzzle follows this arc:
1. **Hook** — Character, setting, stakes (10 words)
2. **Setup** — Rules of the world (20 words)
3. **Puzzle** — What must the solver determine? (15 words)
4. **Misdirection** — The obvious answer that doesn't work
5. **Insight** — The "aha!" that resolves it
6. **Delight** — The satisfaction of seeing it all click
