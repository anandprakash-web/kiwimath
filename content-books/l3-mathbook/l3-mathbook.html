Knowing Your Numbers

Number Theory · Number Sense Core
BIG QUESTIONRoughly how many heartbeats have you had since you were born? Take a guess… then let's find out together. The answer is a number so big it barely fits on the page — and by the end of this chapter you'll read it, write it, and round it like an expert.
🥝
Hi explorer, I'm Kiwi! 🧭 Welcome to Level 3. You already met numbers in Level 2 — now we go bigger, meet brand-new kinds of numbers, and learn the secret tricks the experts use. We'll never jump ahead; every new idea grows out of one you already know. Let's warm up where you're strong: place value.

A 30-second warm-up: place value still rules

Every digit's worth depends on its place. Pull the number 6,408 apart and you can see exactly what each digit is really worth — this is its expanded form:

Notice the 0 in the tens place. It's not 'nothing' — it's a placeholder that keeps the 4 in the hundreds and the 6 in the thousands. Remove it and 6408 collapses into 648. Zeros are the quiet heroes of our number system.

Going bigger: thousands, lakhs, millions

Past the thousands, the places keep growing ten times each step. Two naming systems share the very same number — they just group the digits differently:

• Indian system: group from the right as 2,54,178 and read “two lakh fifty-four thousand one hundred seventy-eight”.
• International system: group in threes — 254,178 — and read “two hundred fifty-four thousand, one hundred seventy-eight”.

The same digits are just grouped differently. After the thousands the two systems part ways — see them side by side:

Same number, two outfits. In the Indian system the big jumps are lakh and crore: a lakh is 1,00,000 = one hundred thousand, and a crore is 1,00,00,000 = one hundred lakh (that's ten million). Keep both in your toolkit — Olympiads use both.

  2. Places: 7 ten-lakhs, 0 lakhs, 0 ten-thousands, 5 thousands, 3 hundreds, 0 tens, 2 ones.
  3. Read it: “seventy lakh five thousand three hundred two.”
  4. The two zeros in the lakhs and ten-thousands places are placeholders — they keep the 7 way up in the ten-lakhs seat.

Comparing & ordering big numbers

To compare, first count digits — more digits wins. Same digit-count? Line them up and compare from the left; the first place that differs decides it.

Your turn. Put these in ascending order: 90,899 · 9,899 · 90,999 · 9,999.
Show answer
Count digits first: 9,899 and 9,999 are 4-digit (smaller). Order: 9,899 < 9,999 < 90,899 < 90,999.

The expert move: rounding & estimating

Experts rarely compute the exact answer first — they estimate to know what to expect, then check. To round, look at the digit just to the right of the place you're rounding to: 5 or more rounds up, 4 or less stays the same.

✏️ Worked example — estimate 612 × 39 in your head
  1. Round each number to something friendly: 612 ≈ 600, 39 ≈ 40.
  2. Multiply the easy numbers: 600 × 40 = 24,000.
  3. So the real answer should be near 24,000. (It's exactly 23,868 — our estimate nailed the size!)
  4. Surprise power: in two seconds you knew the answer wasn't 2,400 or 240,000. Estimation is your built-in mistake-detector.
🥝
Golden habit: estimate first, compute second, then compare. If your exact answer is far from your estimate, you probably slipped — go back and check.

Meet the Integers

Number Theory · Integers Core
BIG QUESTIONThe coldest natural place on Earth once hit about 89 degrees below zero. A submarine can dive to 240 metres below the sea. You might owe a friend 5 rupees. Plain whole numbers can't say any of these — they all need a number that is less than nothing. Do such numbers even exist? By the end of this chapter you'll add and subtract them in your head — and uncover why two minuses make a plus.
🥝
Welcome back, explorer! 🧭 In Chapter 1 our number line started at 0 and marched right: 0, 1, 2, 3 … But what lives to the left of zero? Today we extend the line in the other direction and meet the integers. Nothing here is hard — we'll build every step from a picture you can see.

Why zero can't be the end of the line

Think about a lift (elevator) in a tall building. The ground floor is 0. Going up gives floors 1, 2, 3 … But many buildings have a basement below the ground floor — a car park, maybe two levels down. What number should those floors get? We can't reuse 1 and 2 (those are up). We need new numbers below zero: −1, −2, −3 — read “negative one, negative two”.

Negatives show up everywhere two opposite directions meet a starting point:

• Temperature: above 0° is +, below 0° is −.
• Sea level: a mountain top is +2,000 m, the sea floor is −800 m.
• Money: ₹50 saved is +50; ₹20 owed is −20.
• Time: 3 years from now is +3, 3 years ago is −3.

🥝
The integers are the whole numbers together with their negatives, plus zero:
… −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3 … Zero is the only integer that is neither positive nor negative — it's the perfect middle, the hinge of the whole line.

The integer number line: zero gets a mirror

Take Chapter 1's number line and reflect it through 0. Every positive number now has a twin the same distance to the left. These twins are called opposites: the opposite of 3 is −3, and the opposite of −3 is 3. Zero is its own opposite.

Two rock-solid rules you can read straight off the line:

  1. Bigger is to the right, smaller is to the left. Always. So −2 > −5, because −2 is farther right.
  2. Every negative number is less than every positive number, and both are arranged around 0.

This fixes a famous trap. Which is colder, −2° or −5°? On the line, −5 is farther left than −2, so −5 < −2: −5° is the colder day, even though “5 looks bigger than 2”. The minus sign flips your intuition — the line never lies.

Your turn. Put in order, smallest first: 3, −7, 0, −1, 5.
Show answer
Smallest is the farthest left: −7 < −1 < 0 < 3 < 5.

Absolute value: how FAR from zero (forget the direction)

Sometimes we only care how far a number is from 0, not which side. That distance is its absolute value, written with two straight bars: |−5| means “the distance of −5 from 0”, which is 5. Distance is never negative, so an absolute value is never negative.

✏️ Worked example — compute |−7|, |3|, |0|, and the opposite of −9
  1. |−7| = 7 (it's 7 steps from 0).
  2. |3| = 3 (3 steps from 0).
  3. |0| = 0 (0 is sitting right on 0).
  4. The opposite of −9 is +9 — same distance, other side. (Don't confuse: |−9| = 9 is a distance; the opposite of −9 is the number 9.)
🥝
Picture absolute value as a footstep-counter: stand on the number, walk to 0, count steps. −6 and 6 give the same count, 6. It throws away the sign and keeps only the size.
Your turn. Which is greater: |−8| or |5|? And which number is greater, −8 or 5?
Show answer
|−8| = 8 > |5| = 5, so |−8| is greater. But as numbers, −8 < 5 (−8 is far left). Size and position are different questions!

Adding integers — just keep walking on the line

Adding is moving along the line. Adding a positive walks you right; adding a negative walks you left. Start where the first number says, then take the second number's walk.

✏️ Worked example — (+3) + (−5)
  1. Start at +3.
  2. Add −5 → walk 5 steps LEFT.
  3. 3 → 2 → 1 → 0 → −1 → −2. You land on −2.

Here's a second way to picture it — the chip model. Let an orange + chip and a blue − chip cancel to nothing (a “zero pair”). To compute 3 + (−5), put out 3 plus-chips and 5 minus-chips and remove every pair that cancels:

Both pictures agree: (+3) + (−5) = −2. Use whichever you like — the number line for movement, the chips for cancelling.

Two quick patterns fall out for free:

  1. Same signs: add the sizes, keep the sign. (−4) + (−3): four left then three more left = −7.
  2. Different signs: subtract the smaller size from the bigger, keep the sign of the bigger. (+8) + (−3): 8 beats 3 by 5, and 8 is positive → +5.
Your turn. Compute (−6) + (+10) and (−2) + (−9).
Show answer
(−6) + (+10): different signs, 10 beats 6 by 4, positive wins → +4. (−2) + (−9): same sign, 2 + 9 = 11, both negative → −11.

Subtracting integers — the “add the opposite” secret

Subtraction has a beautiful shortcut. Subtracting a number is the same as adding its opposite. In symbols: a − b = a + (−b). Why? Because “take away 5” and “add −5” move you the same way on the line — both walk 5 steps left.

✏️ Worked example — (+4) − (+9) using add-the-opposite
  1. Rewrite: (+4) − (+9) = (+4) + (−9).
  2. Now it's an addition. Different signs: 9 beats 4 by 5, and 9 is the negative one.
  3. So the answer is −5. (Check on the line: start at 4, walk 9 left, land on −5. ✓)

This is exactly why a thermometer dropping from 4° by 9 degrees reads −5°. The everyday word “drop” is subtraction, and the integer machine handles it without blinking.

Your turn. Compute (−3) − (+7) and 6 − (−2). (Add the opposite each time.)
Show answer
(−3) − (+7) = (−3) + (−7) = −10. 6 − (−2) = 6 + (+2) = +8 (subtracting a negative adds — that's the next big surprise!).

🤯 The big surprise: why two minuses make a plus

🥝
Everyone is told “minus times minus is plus” and “subtracting a negative adds.” But why? Let me show you, not just tell you. Two little stories, no memorising.

Story 1 — the debt eraser. Subtracting means “take away.” A negative number is a debt — something you owe. So −(−2) means “take away a ₹2 debt.” If I erase ₹2 of debt you owe, you are ₹2 richer — that's the same as giving you ₹2. So removing a negative is the same as adding a positive: 6 − (−2) = 6 + 2 = 8.

Story 2 — let the pattern force the answer. Watch a staircase of subtractions where the second number drops by 1 each line. The answers must keep climbing by 1 — patterns don't suddenly break:

The pattern forces 5 − (−1) = 6 and 5 − (−2) = 7. Subtracting a negative adds. Both stories land in the same place: two minus signs sitting together turn into a plus.

🥝
Keep this jingle: “minus a minus is a plus.” a − (−b) = a + b. You didn't memorise it — you watched it have to be true. That's real maths power. 🎉
✏️ Worked example — simplify −12 + (−98) − (−84) + (−7)
  1. Turn every subtraction into add-the-opposite: −12 + (−98) + (+84) + (−7).
  2. Gather the negatives: (−12) + (−98) + (−7) = −117.
  3. Add the positive: −117 + 84.
  4. Different signs: 117 beats 84 by 33, negative wins → −33.

Apply
  1. A diver at −18 m rises 25 m. What's her new depth?
    answer −18 + 25 = +7 m (7 m ABOVE sea level — she has surfaced).
  2. The temperature is −3°C and falls 6°. Now what?
    answer −3 + (−6) = −9°C.
  3. Compute (−7) + (−8) + 20.
    answer (−15) + 20 = +5.
  4. You owe ₹40 (that's −40) and pay back ₹25. What's your balance?
    answer −40 + 25 = −15 (you still owe ₹15).
  5. Evaluate 12 − 19 − (−4).
    answer 12 + (−19) + (+4) = −3.
Analyze
  1. From the sum of 6 and 12, subtract the difference of −15 and −7. What's the result?
    answer Sum 6 + 12 = 18. Difference (−15) − (−7) = −15 + 7 = −8. Then 18 − (−8) = 18 + 8 = 26.
  2. Two integers are opposites and their absolute values are 9. What could they be, and what's their sum?
    answer 9 and −9; opposites always add to 0.
  3. Find an integer x with |x| = 6 and x < 0.
    answer x = −6.
  4. Which is greater and by how much: (−5) + (−6) or (−2) − (+9)?
    answer (−5)+(−6) = −11. (−2)−(+9) = −11. They're EQUAL — neither is greater.
Create
  1. Invent a real-life story that ends at exactly −4 using one rise and one fall.
    answer E.g. start at +5°, drop 9° → −4°. Or: in a lift, go up 2 then down 6 from floor 0 → −4.
  2. Write three different integer additions that all equal −7.
    answer Many work: (−3)+(−4); (−10)+(+3); 0+(−7); (−12)+(+5).
  3. Build a subtraction of two negatives whose answer is positive, and check it on the line.
    answer E.g. (−2) − (−9) = −2 + 9 = +7. ✓

Divisibility, Factors & Primes

Number Theory · Prime Hunters Stretch
BIG QUESTIONIs 91 a prime number? It looks like one — it's odd, it doesn't end in 0 or 5, and nothing obvious divides it. Most people guess “prime.” They're wrong, and by the end of this chapter you'll spot exactly why in about three seconds. You'll also meet a jaw-dropping rule: HCF × LCM = the product of the two numbers, every single time.
🥝
Hello again, number detective! 🔍 Every whole number is secretly built out of smaller pieces, the way a wall is built from bricks. Today we learn to x-ray a number: find what divides it, which numbers are the unbreakable ‘atoms’ (primes), and how to compare two numbers' insides to find what they share. We start from something you already own — multiplication.

Factors and multiples: two sides of one coin

12 = 3 × 4. We say 3 and 4 are factors of 12 (they divide it with no remainder), and 12 is a multiple of 3 and of 4. A factor fits inside a number exactly; a multiple is what you get by skip-counting the number. Picture 12 as a perfect rectangle — every way to make a full rectangle gives a factor pair:

To list all factors of a number, hunt in pairs from the outside in. For 12: 1×12, 2×6, 3×4 — then 4 already appeared, so stop. Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Every number has 1 and itself as factors; the interesting ones live in between.

✏️ Worked example — find all factors of 36
  1. Hunt in pairs: 1×36, 2×18, 3×12, 4×9, 6×6.
  2. After 6×6 the pairs start repeating, so stop.
  3. Factors of 36: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36 — nine of them.
  4. Notice 6×6: when a number is a perfect square its middle factor pairs into itself.
Your turn. List all factors of 24.
Show answer
1×24, 2×12, 3×8, 4×6 → 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24 (eight factors).

Divisibility magic: knowing WITHOUT dividing

Long division is slow. There are lightning shortcuts that tell you if a number is divisible by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10 or 11 — just by glancing at its digits. Here's your reference card; we'll prove the two coolest ones below.

🥝
The 3-and-9 rule feels like magic: why should adding the digits tell you anything? Because 10 = 9 + 1, 100 = 99 + 1, 1000 = 999 + 1 … and 9, 99, 999 are all multiples of 9. So a number is ‘a pile of 9s’ plus its digit-sum. The pile of 9s is already divisible by 9 (and by 3), so the leftover — the digit-sum — decides it. Beautiful, not magic!
✏️ Worked example — is 9,724 divisible by 6? (the source-PDF puzzle)
  1. By 6 means by 2 AND by 3.
  2. By 2: last digit 4 is even — yes. ✓
  3. By 3: digit-sum 9+7+2+4 = 22, and 22 is not a multiple of 3 — no. ✗
  4. Since it fails the 3-test, 9,724 is not divisible by 6.
  5. Bonus: to make 9724* divisible by 6, the * must be even AND make the digit-sum a multiple of 3. Digit-sum so far is 22; +2 gives 24 (a multiple of 3) and 2 is even → * = 2 works.
✏️ Worked example — test 3,4,5,9 on 4,950
  1. By 5: ends in 0 — yes. By 10: ends in 0 — yes too.
  2. By 9: digit-sum 4+9+5+0 = 18, a multiple of 9 — yes (so by 3 as well).
  3. By 4: last two digits 50, and 50 is not a multiple of 4 — no.
  4. So 4,950 is divisible by 3, 5, 9, 10 but not by 4.
Your turn. Is 8,261,955 divisible by 11? (Use the alternating-sum rule, right to left.)
Show answer
Alternating sum: 5−5+9−1+6−2+8 = 20. 20 is not 0 or a multiple of 11, so no. (Adding 2 would make it 22 — a multiple of 11 — which is why the smallest number to add is 2.)

Primes: the unbreakable atoms of arithmetic

A number with exactly two factors — 1 and itself — is prime: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 … A number with more than two factors is composite: 4, 6, 8, 9, 10 … The number 1 is special — it has only one factor (itself), so it's neither prime nor composite. And 2 is the only even prime — every other even number has 2 as an extra factor.

Here's the detective trick that cracks our opening puzzle. To test if a number is prime, you only need to try dividing by primes up to its square root — because if it had a factor bigger than its square root, the matching partner would be smaller, and you'd have caught that already.

✏️ Worked example — is 91 prime? (the trap from page 1)
  1. √91 is a bit less than 10 (since 10×10 = 100), so test primes up to 9: 2, 3, 5, 7.
  2. Not even (so not 2); digit-sum 10 (so not 3); doesn't end in 0/5 (so not 5).
  3. Try 7: 7 × 13 = 91. Found a factor!
  4. So 91 = 7 × 13 is composite, not prime. The trap is that 91 has no small factor, so it ‘feels’ prime — but 7 × 13 was hiding in plain sight.
Your turn. Is 97 prime? Test primes up to 9.
Show answer
2,3,5,7 all fail to divide 97 (97 is odd, digit-sum 16, doesn't end in 0/5, and 97/7 isn't whole). So 97 is prime.

Prime factorization: every number's secret recipe

The grand theorem of arithmetic: every whole number above 1 is a product of primes in exactly one way (apart from the order). That unique recipe is its prime factorization. We find it with a factor tree — keep splitting until every leaf is prime.

Collect the leaves: 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 = 2² × 3 × 5. Surprise check: it doesn't matter how you start the tree. Split 60 as 4 × 15 instead and you still land on 2² × 3 × 5. The recipe is destiny.

✏️ Worked example — prime-factorize 72
  1. 72 = 8 × 9.
  2. 8 = 2 × 2 × 2, and 9 = 3 × 3.
  3. So 72 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 2³ × 3².
  4. Quick brick-count: that's three 2-bricks and two 3-bricks — the complete blueprint of 72.
Your turn. Write the prime factorization of 84.
Show answer
84 = 4 × 21 = (2×2) × (3×7) = 2² × 3 × 7.

HCF and LCM: comparing two numbers' insides

Lay two numbers' prime recipes side by side and two famous numbers appear:

  1. HCF (Highest Common Factor, also called GCD) = the primes they share — the biggest number that divides both.
  2. LCM (Lowest Common Multiple) = the smallest number that both divide into — you take every prime to its highest power seen.

Take 12 = 2² × 3 and 18 = 2 × 3². A Venn diagram makes the sharing visible — shared prime-bricks go in the middle, leftovers go outside:

✏️ Worked example — find HCF and LCM of 12 and 18
  1. 12 = 2 × 2 × 3. 18 = 2 × 3 × 3.
  2. Shared bricks (take the lower power of each common prime): one 2 and one 3 → HCF = 2 × 3 = 6.
  3. For LCM take the higher power of every prime that appears: 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = 36.
  4. Sense-check: 6 divides both 12 and 18 (yes), and 36 is the first number both 12 and 18 march into.
🥝
Memory hook: HCF is Hidden inside — the part both share. LCM is Large enough to hold both. HCF ≤ either number ≤ LCM, always.
Your turn. Find the HCF and LCM of 8 and 12.
Show answer
8 = 2³, 12 = 2²×3. HCF = 2² = 4; LCM = 2³×3 = 24.
✏️ Worked example — four bells ring every 6, 8 and 9 seconds — when do all ring together?
  1. “Together again” = the LCM of 6, 8, 9.
  2. 6 = 2×3, 8 = 2³, 9 = 3².
  3. Highest power of each prime: 2³ × 3² = 8 × 9 = 72.
  4. So all three ring together every 72 seconds — LCM is the ‘everyone-meets’ number.

✨ The surprise: HCF × LCM = the product of the numbers

Look back at 12 and 18. HCF = 6, LCM = 36. Now multiply them: 6 × 36 = 216. And 12 × 18 = 216. The same number! This is no accident:

Why must it be true? In the Venn picture, the HCF is the shared bricks and the LCM is all the bricks. When you multiply HCF × LCM you use the shared bricks twice and the leftover bricks once — which is exactly the bricks of the first number times the bricks of the second. So HCF × LCM = a × b. It can never fail.

🥝
This gives a super shortcut: if you know the HCF, you get the LCM for free — LCM = (a × b) ÷ HCF. No need to build the LCM brick by brick!
✏️ Worked example — the LCM of two numbers is 30 and their HCF is 15; one number is 30 — find the other (source-PDF Q7)
  1. Use HCF × LCM = a × b → 15 × 30 = 30 × b.
  2. So 450 = 30 × b.
  3. b = 450 ÷ 30 = 15.
  4. Check: HCF(30, 15) = 15 ✓ and LCM(30, 15) = 30 ✓. The shortcut nailed it.

Powers & Exponents

Number Theory · Powers Stretch
BIG QUESTIONFold a sheet of paper in half, then in half again, and again… If you could fold it just 42 times, how thick would the stack be? A few centimetres? A metre? The real answer is past the Moon. That is the secret power of doubling — and by the end of this chapter you'll command it with a tiny raised number called an exponent.
🥝
Hi again, explorer! ⚡ In Chapter 3 we multiplied the same prime again and again — like 2 × 2 × 2. Writing all those is tiring. Mathematicians invented a beautiful shorthand: the exponent. It looks small but it is mighty. Let's build it from what you already know — plain multiplication.

Exponents: a shortcut for repeated multiplication

Just as 4 × 3 is shorthand for ‘add 4 three times,’ an exponent is shorthand for ‘multiply the same number several times.’ We write 2 × 2 × 2 as 2³. The big number is the base (what we multiply), the little raised number is the exponent or power (how many copies).

Careful — an exponent is not multiplication of the two numbers. 2³ is 8, not 2 × 3 = 6. The exponent only counts how many copies of the base to multiply.

✏️ Worked example — write and evaluate ‘five to the power 3’ and ‘ten to the power 4’
  1. Five to the power 3 = 5³ = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125.
  2. Ten to the power 4 = 10⁴ = 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000.
  3. Notice 10⁴ is 1 followed by 4 zeros — powers of ten are wonderfully tidy. More on that soon.
Your turn. Write 7 × 7 × 7 × 7 in exponent form and find its value.
Show answer
7⁴ = 2,401.

Squares and cubes: powers you can SEE

Two powers are so common they earned nicknames. n² is read ‘n squared’ — because n×n is the number of dots in a real square. n³ is ‘n cubed’ — the number of little cubes that fill a real cube.

The first few perfect squares are worth knowing by heart: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100. The first few perfect cubes: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125. (Did you spot that 64 is in both lists? 64 = 8² = 4³ — a number can be a square and a cube at once!)

Your turn. What is 6², and what is 4³? Which is bigger?
Show answer
6² = 36, 4³ = 64. 4³ is bigger — cubing grows faster than squaring.

Square roots: undoing the square

If squaring takes 7 to 49, the square root goes back: it asks ‘what number, squared, gives this?’ We write it √49 = 7, because 7² = 49. Square-rooting is the opposite of squaring, the way subtraction undoes addition.

To find a square root of a perfect square, hunt for the number that makes it. Knowing your squares makes this instant: √81 = 9, √144 = 12. A neat trick uses prime factorization: pair up the primes and take one from each pair.

✏️ Worked example — find √324 using prime factorization
  1. 324 = 4 × 81 = 2² × 3⁴.
  2. Make pairs: (2×2) and (3×3) and (3×3).
  3. Take one number from each pair: 2 × 3 × 3 = 18.
  4. So √324 = 18. Check: 18² = 324. ✓
Your turn. Find √196.
Show answer
196 = 14 × 14, so √196 = 14. (Or 196 = 2²×7², take 2×7 = 14.)

Algebra · Parts & Wholes Core
BIG QUESTIONTwo pizzas, one cut into 4 slices and one into 6. You grab 3 slices from each. Did you take the same amount of pizza both times? And here's the twist — could you write each amount as a number with a tiny dot in it instead? By the end of this chapter you'll answer both in your head.

Welcome back, explorer! 🥝 In Level 2 you met fractions (parts of a whole) and decimals (the dot way of writing them). This chapter is your power-up: we'll sharpen everything you know, then add the grown-up moves — comparing, all four operations, mixed numbers, rounding, and the secret bridge that turns any fraction into a decimal and back. Nothing new will appear out of thin air; each move grows from one you already trust.

30-second warm-up: a fraction is two jobs in one

Every fraction does two jobs at once. The bottom number (the denominator) says how many equal parts the whole was cut into. The top number (the numerator) counts how many of those parts you're talking about. Here are 3 shaded out of 8 equal pieces:

Same idea on a round pizza — 5 slices out of 6 is 5/6:

🥝
Remember the surprise from Level 2: a bigger denominator means smaller pieces. So 1/8 is smaller than 1/4, even though 8 is bigger than 4. Cutting into more pieces makes each piece tinier.

Equivalent fractions — same amount, different name

Cut a bar in half and shade one half: 1/2. Now slice each half in two so the bar has 4 pieces — the shaded amount is now 2 of 4. Not one crumb of chocolate moved, so 1/2 = 2/4. Fractions that name the same amount are equivalent.

The rule: multiply (or divide) the top and the bottom by the same number, and the fraction keeps its value. Multiplying changes its name, never its amount.

Going the other way — dividing top and bottom — gives the simplest form: the smallest, cleanest name a fraction can have. You divide both by their biggest common factor until nothing else divides evenly.

✏️ Worked example — write 18/24 in simplest form
  1. Find a number that divides both 18 and 24. The biggest one is 6.
  2. Divide top and bottom by 6: (18÷6)/(24÷6) = 3/4.
  3. Can 3/4 be cut down more? 3 and 4 share no factor but 1 — so 3/4 is simplest. ✓
  4. Check: 3/4 = 6/8 = 9/12 = 18/24, all the same slice of the world.
Your turn. Write 6/10 in simplest form.
Show answer
Both 6 and 10 divide by 2: (6÷2)/(10÷2) = 3/5. (And 3 and 5 share nothing, so we're done.)

Comparing & ordering fractions

If two fractions already share the same denominator, just compare the tops: 5/8 beats 3/8 because 5 pieces beat 3 pieces of the same size. When the denominators are different, give the fractions a common denominator first — rename them so the pieces are the same size, then compare.

✏️ Worked example — which is bigger, 2/3 or 3/4?
  1. The denominators are 3 and 4. A size that fits both is 12.
  2. Rename: 2/3 = 8/12 (×4 top and bottom). And 3/4 = 9/12 (×3 top and bottom).
  3. Now compare the tops: 9 > 8, so 3/4 > 2/3.
  4. Picture-check: three-quarters is closer to a whole than two-thirds. ✓
Your turn. Order from smallest to biggest: 1/2, 2/5, 7/10.
Show answer
Use tenths: 1/2 = 5/10, 2/5 = 4/10, 7/10 = 7/10. So 2/5 < 1/2 < 7/10.

Adding & subtracting fractions

You can only add or subtract things that are the same size. Three apples plus two apples is five apples — but three apples plus two oranges isn't five of anything. Fractions are the same: make the denominators match, then add (or subtract) just the tops. The denominator names the piece size, so it stays put.

✏️ Worked example — add 2/3 + 1/4
  1. Common denominator of 3 and 4 is 12.
  2. Rename: 2/3 = 8/12 and 1/4 = 3/12.
  3. Add the tops, keep the bottom: 8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12.
  4. Is 11/12 simplest? 11 and 12 share no factor — yes. ✓
✏️ Worked example — subtract 3/4 − 5/8
  1. Common denominator of 4 and 8 is 8.
  2. Rename only the one that needs it: 3/4 = 6/8.
  3. Subtract the tops: 6/8 − 5/8 = 1/8. ✓
Your turn. Work out 5/6 − 1/3.
Show answer
Common bottom 6: 1/3 = 2/6. Then 5/6 − 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2 in simplest form.
⚠️ Common mistake

A super-common slip: 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/6. Here the bottoms got added too — but the denominator is the piece size, and that doesn't change when you collect pieces! Worse, 2/6 = 1/3, the same as one of the parts you added — a sum can't equal just one of its pieces. The right way: the bottoms already match, so add only the tops and keep the bottom: 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/3. (Think: one-third pizza plus one-third pizza is two-thirds of a pizza.)

Multiplying fractions — and the magic word “of”

Multiplying fractions is the easy one — no common denominator needed! Just multiply the tops together and the bottoms together, then simplify.

✏️ Worked example — multiply 2/3 × 6/7
  1. Tops: 2 × 6 = 12. Bottoms: 3 × 7 = 21. So 12/21.
  2. Simplify: 12 and 21 both divide by 3 → 4/7. ✓
🥝
Here's a surprise that unlocks half of word-problem maths: the word “of” means multiply. “Two-fifths of 30” is just 2/5 × 30. And the quick trick — divide by the bottom, multiply by the top: 30 ÷ 5 = 6, then 6 × 2 = 12.
✏️ Worked example — find 3/8 of 64
  1. Divide by the bottom: 64 ÷ 8 = 8 (that's one-eighth).
  2. Multiply by the top: 8 × 3 = 24.
  3. So 3/8 of 64 = 24. (Check: 5/8 would be the other 40, and 24 + 40 = 64. ✓)
Your turn. A class has 48 pupils and 5/6 of them came to the trip. How many came?
Show answer
48 ÷ 6 = 8, then 8 × 5 = 40 pupils came. (The 8 who stayed back are the missing 1/6.)

Dividing fractions — flip and multiply

Dividing by a fraction asks: “how many of these fit inside?” And here is the famous shortcut: keep the first, flip the second, then multiply. (The flipped fraction is called its reciprocal — top and bottom swapped.)

✏️ Worked example — work out 3/4 ÷ 2/3
  1. Keep the first: 3/4.
  2. Flip the second: 2/3 becomes 3/2.
  3. Multiply: 3/4 × 3/2 = 9/8 = 1⅛. ✓
  4. Why flip works: dividing by 2/3 is the same as multiplying by how-many-2/3-make-1, which is 3/2.
Your turn. How many 1/4-cups of flour are in 3 cups?
Show answer
3 ÷ 1/4 = 3 × 4 = 12 quarter-cups. (Each whole cup holds 4 quarter-cups, and 3 × 4 = 12.)

Mixed numbers ↔ improper fractions

A mixed number like 1¾ means “one whole and three quarters.” An improper fraction like 7/4 means “seven quarter-pieces.” They're the same amount wearing different clothes — and you can switch between them in your head.

• Improper → mixed: divide top by bottom. 7 ÷ 4 = 1 remainder 3, so 7/4 = 1¾.
• Mixed → improper: multiply the whole by the bottom, add the top. For 2⅜: 2 × 5 + 3 = 13, so 2⅜ = 13/5.

✏️ Worked example — add the mixed numbers 1½ + 2¾
  1. Turn each into an improper fraction: 1½ = 3/2 and 2¾ = 11/4.
  2. Common denominator 4: 3/2 = 6/4. Add: 6/4 + 11/4 = 17/4.
  3. Back to a mixed number: 17 ÷ 4 = 4 remainder 1 → 4¼. ✓
Your turn. Change 11/4 to a mixed number, then change 3½ to an improper fraction.
Show answer
11 ÷ 4 = 2 r3 → 2¾. And 3½ = (3×2 + 1)/2 = 7/2.

Decimals: place value to the thousandths

A decimal is a fraction whose bottom is always 10, 100, 1000… — a power of ten. The tiny decimal point separates the wholes (on the left) from the parts (on the right). Each place to the right is worth ten times less than the one before: tenths, then hundredths, then thousandths.

Read 4.276 place by place: 4 ones + 2 tenths + 7 hundredths + 6 thousandths, the same as 4 + 2/10 + 7/100 + 6/1000. Out loud it's “four point two seven six.”

🥝
Big idea you'll use forever: extra zeros on the right of a decimal don't change its value. 0.5 = 0.50 = 0.500 — five tenths, fifty hundredths, five hundred thousandths are all the same amount. That trick makes comparing and subtracting decimals painless.
Your turn. In 3.408, what is the value of the 4, and what is the value of the 8?
Show answer
The 4 is in the tenths place → 4 tenths = 0.4. The 8 is in the thousandths place → 8 thousandths = 0.008.

Adding & subtracting decimals — line up the dots

The one golden rule: line up the decimal points (which lines up tenths under tenths, hundredths under hundredths), pad with zeros so both have the same length, then add or subtract just like whole numbers and bring the dot straight down.

✏️ Worked example — add 0.7 + 0.45
  1. Pad: 0.7 becomes 0.70 so both have two places.
  2. Line up the dots and add: 0.70 + 0.45 = 1.15.
  3. Check it's sensible: 0.7 is a bit over a half, 0.45 a bit under, so just over 1. ✓
✏️ Worked example — subtract 3.6 − 1.85
  1. Pad: 3.6 becomes 3.60.
  2. Line up and subtract: 3.60 − 1.85 = 1.75. ✓
Your turn. A ribbon is 2.5 m long and you cut off 0.8 m. How much is left?
Show answer
2.5 − 0.8 = 1.7 m left.

Multiplying & dividing decimals

Multiplying: ignore the dots, multiply as whole numbers, then count the total decimal places in both factors and put that many places in the answer. Dividing: the neat trick is to make the divider a whole number by shifting both dots the same number of places.

✏️ Worked example — multiply 1.2 × 0.3
  1. Ignore dots: 12 × 3 = 36.
  2. Count decimal places: 1.2 has 1, and 0.3 has 1 → 2 places in total.
  3. Put 2 places in the answer: 0.36.
  4. Sanity check: about 1 of 0.3 is 0.3, so a bit more — 0.36 fits. ✓
✏️ Worked example — divide 4.5 ÷ 5
  1. Divider 5 is already a whole number, so divide straight: 4.5 ÷ 5.
  2. 45 ÷ 5 = 9, and there's 1 decimal place, so the answer is 0.9. ✓
Your turn. Work out 0.8 ÷ 0.2.
Show answer
Shift both dots one place to make the divider whole: 8 ÷ 2 = 4. (How many 0.2s fit in 0.8? Four of them.)

Rounding decimals

To round to a place, look at the digit just to its right: 5 or more rounds the place up, 4 or less leaves it the same — then drop the rest. This is how prices, measurements and scores get tidied up.

✏️ Worked example — round 3.847 to 2 decimal places
  1. We're keeping 2 places: 3.84… The digit to the right is the 7 in the thousandths.
  2. 7 is 5-or-more, so round the hundredths up: 4 becomes 5.
  3. Answer: 3.85. ✓
Your turn. Round 12.96 to 1 decimal place.
Show answer
The digit after the tenths is 6 (≥ 5), so the tenths 9 rounds up — it carries: 12.96 → 13.0.

The bridge: fraction ↔ decimal

Fractions and decimals are the same idea in two outfits — and crossing between them is a superpower. Fraction → decimal: just divide the top by the bottom (or rename the bottom to 10/100/1000). Decimal → fraction: count the places — two places means hundredths — then simplify.

A few worth memorising forever: 1/2 = 0.5, 1/4 = 0.25, 3/4 = 0.75, 1/5 = 0.2, 1/10 = 0.1, 1/8 = 0.125.

✏️ Worked example — write 3/8 as a decimal
  1. Divide top by bottom: 3 ÷ 8.
  2. 3.000 ÷ 8 = 0.375 (8 into 30 is 3 with 6 left, into 60 is 7 with 4 left, into 40 is 5 exactly).
  3. So 3/8 = 0.375. ✓
✏️ Worked example — write 0.375 back as a fraction
  1. It has 3 decimal places → thousandths: 0.375 = 375/1000.
  2. Simplify: both divide by 125 → 3/8.
  3. Full circle — 3/8 and 0.375 are the same number. ✓
🥝
A delicious surprise: not every fraction gives a tidy decimal. Try 1/3 — divide and you get 0.3333… forever! These never-ending decimals are called recurring decimals, and we write them with a dot or bar above the repeating digit. The fraction 1/3 is the exact value; the decimal can only get close. Sometimes a fraction is the more honest number. 🎯
Your turn. Write 7/20 as a decimal.
Show answer
Rename the bottom to 100: 7/20 = 35/100 (×5 top and bottom) = 0.35.

Percentages — the % Idea

Algebra · Parts & Wholes Core
BIG QUESTIONTwo video games are on sale. One shouts “40% OFF!” and costs ₹500 before the cut. The other says “SAVE ₹150” and costs ₹400 before the cut. Which sale actually saves you more money? The bigger-looking number isn't always the better deal — and percentages are how you see through the trick.
🥝
Hi explorer! 🥝 You already know fractions and decimals are two outfits for the same parts-of-a-whole idea. Get ready for the third outfit: the percent. It's the one you'll meet most in real life — sale tags, battery icons, test scores, phone storage, exam results — and it's secretly the friendliest of all, because it always uses the same whole: 100.

Percent just means “out of 100”

The word per-cent literally means “per hundred” — out of 100. The little sign % is just a fancy way of writing “/100”. So 37% means 37 out of 100, the fraction 37/100, the decimal 0.37. Same amount, three outfits.

🥝
This is why percent is so handy: imagine every whole is cut into 100 equal squares, always. Then a percent is simply “how many squares.” 50% is half the grid, 25% is a quarter, 100% is the whole grid, and — surprise — you can even go past 100% when you have more than one whole!

Some you'll just know on sight, like old friends:

Your turn. Shade-in your head: how many squares is 10%, and what fraction is that?
Show answer
10% = 10 of 100 squares = 10/100 = 1/10. (And as a decimal, 0.1.)

The three outfits: percent ↔ fraction ↔ decimal

Crossing between the three is fast once you remember % means /100:

• Percent → fraction: put it over 100 and simplify. 40% = 40/100 = 2/5.
• Percent → decimal: divide by 100 — shift the dot two places left. 37% = 0.37, and 8% = 0.08.
• Decimal → percent: multiply by 100 — shift the dot two places right. 0.6 = 60%.
• Fraction → percent: first make it a decimal (divide top by bottom), then ×100. 3/4 = 0.75 = 75%.

✏️ Worked example — turn 2/5 into a percent
  1. Make it a decimal: 2 ÷ 5 = 0.4.
  2. Multiply by 100 (shift dot two right): 0.4 → 40%.
  3. Check with the grid idea: 2/5 of 100 squares = 40 squares = 40%. ✓
✏️ Worked example — turn 12% into a fraction and a decimal
  1. Fraction: 12/100. Both divide by 4 → 3/25.
  2. Decimal: 12 ÷ 100 = 0.12.
  3. So 12% = 3/25 = 0.12 — one amount, three names. ✓
🥝
Watch the surprise hiding in 1/8. As a decimal it's 0.125, so as a percent it's 12.5% — percentages are allowed to have a decimal point! And 1/3 becomes the never-ending 33.33…%, often written as 33⅓%. Percent doesn't have to be a whole number.
Your turn. Write 0.09 as a percent, and 75% as a fraction in simplest form.
Show answer
0.09 × 100 = 9%. And 75% = 75/100 = 3/4.

Finding a percent OF a quantity

Here's the move you'll use most: finding a percent of something — “20% of 50 marbles,” “15% of ₹200.” Remember from Ch5 that “of” means multiply. So 20% of 50 = 20/100 × 50. Two friendly ways to do it:

• Fraction way: turn the percent into a fraction, then take that fraction of the number. 20% = 1/5, and 1/5 of 50 = 50 ÷ 5 = 10.
• Decimal way: turn the percent into a decimal and multiply. 0.20 × 50 = 10.

✏️ Worked example — find 15% of ₹200
  1. Decimal way: 15% = 0.15. Then 0.15 × 200 = ₹30.
  2. Or the 10%+5% trick: 10% of 200 = 20, and 5% is half of that = 10. Add: 20 + 10 = ₹30. ✓
  3. So 15% of ₹200 is ₹30. (And the remaining 85% would be ₹170.)
🥝
The secret weapon for mental percentages: 10% is just “move the dot one place left.” 10% of 350 = 35. Need 5%? Halve the 10%. Need 20%? Double it. Need 30%? Triple it. Almost any everyday percentage is a few 10%-jumps stacked together — no calculator required.
✏️ Worked example — find 8% of 250
  1. Decimal way: 8% = 0.08. Then 0.08 × 250 = 20.
  2. Check with 10% − 2%: 10% of 250 = 25; 1% = 2.5, so 2% = 5; 25 − 5 = 20. ✓
Your turn. A test has 80 marks. You scored 75%. How many marks is that?
Show answer
75% of 80 = 0.75 × 80 = 60 marks. (Or 3/4 of 80 = 60.)
⚠️ Common mistake

A trap to dodge: writing “20% of 50 = 20”. The 20 is the percent, not the answer — you still have to take that share of 50. The right way: “of” means multiply, so 20% of 50 = 20/100 × 50 = 10. (Quick check: 20% is one-fifth, and one-fifth of 50 is 50 ÷ 5 = 10. ✓) A percent on its own is just a label until you say percent of what.

Finding what percent one number is of another

Sometimes you have the part and the whole, and you want the percentage — “I got 15 out of 60, what percent is that?” The recipe: part ÷ whole, then × 100. (It's just the part-over-whole fraction, dressed as a percent.)

✏️ Worked example — 15 out of 60 is what percent?
  1. Make the fraction: part over whole = 15/60.
  2. Simplify if you like: 15/60 = 1/4.
  3. Turn into a percent: 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%. (Or straight: 15 ÷ 60 × 100 = 25.) ✓
✏️ Worked example — a phone is charged from empty to 18 of 24 battery-bars. What % is the battery?
  1. Part over whole: 18/24.
  2. Simplify: 18/24 = 3/4.
  3. As a percent: 3/4 = 75%. The battery icon would read 75%. 🔋
Your turn. In a class of 20, twelve pupils wear glasses. What percent wear glasses?
Show answer
12 ÷ 20 × 100 = 60%. (12/20 = 3/5 = 0.6 = 60%.)

Percentage increase & decrease

Prices rise, prices fall, populations grow, scores improve. To change a number by a percent, find that percent of it, then add (for an increase) or subtract (for a decrease).

✏️ Worked example — a ₹200 book goes up by 15%. New price?
  1. Find 15% of 200: 0.15 × 200 = ₹30 (the increase).
  2. Add it on: 200 + 30 = ₹230.
  3. Shortcut: an increase of 15% means you pay 115% of the old price → 1.15 × 200 = ₹230. ✓
✏️ Worked example — an ₹80 toy is 25% off. Sale price?
  1. Find 25% of 80: 1/4 of 80 = ₹20 (the discount).
  2. Take it away: 80 − 20 = ₹60.
  3. Shortcut: 25% off means you pay 75% → 0.75 × 80 = ₹60. ✓
🥝
A famous percentage trap to enjoy: a price goes up 10%, then down 10%. Back to the start? No! Start at 100 → up 10% → 110 → down 10% of 110 (that's 11) → 99. You end up lower! The reason: the second 10% is taken from a bigger number than the first. Percentages always ask “percent of what?” — and the 'what' can change underneath you. 😮
Your turn. A water tank holds 150 litres but is 10% empty. How many litres are in it?
Show answer
10% of 150 = 15 litres missing, so 150 − 15 = 135 litres are in the tank.

Profit, Loss & Discounts

Arithmetic · Money Maths Core
BIG QUESTIONA shopkeeper buys a cricket bat for ₹400 and sells it for ₹500. Easy — she made ₹100. But now two bats: she sells one for a ₹100 gain and the other for a ₹100 gain too… yet she tells you one sale was a better deal than the other. How can the same ₹100 profit be better on one bat than the other? The answer is hiding in a percentage.
🥝
Hello again! 🥝 Last chapter you mastered percentages. Now we spend them in the real world — the world of buying and selling. Every shop runs on a few simple words, and once you know them, you'll never be fooled by a price tag again. Let's set up our shop.

The shopkeeper's three words

Almost every money problem uses just three quantities. Meet them:

• Cost Price (CP) — what the seller paid to get the item.
• Selling Price (SP) — what the seller sold it for.
• Profit or Loss — the difference between them.

The rule writes itself. If you sell for more than you paid, you made a profit; if you sell for less, you took a loss:

Profit = SP − CP  (when SP > CP)   |   Loss = CP − SP  (when CP > SP)

✏️ Worked example — buy at ₹400, sell at ₹500
  1. CP = ₹400 (what she paid). SP = ₹500 (what she sold for).
  2. SP > CP, so it's a profit: Profit = 500 − 400 = ₹100. ✓
Your turn. A toy is bought for ₹250 and sold for ₹200. Profit or loss, and how much?
Show answer
SP < CP, so it's a loss: 250 − 200 = ₹50 loss.

The golden rule: profit% and loss% are ALWAYS on the cost price

Money profit alone can fool you (as our two-bats puzzle showed). To compare deals fairly, we ask: the profit is what percent of what it cost? This is profit%, and there is one rule you must never break:

🥝
⭐ THE GOLDEN RULE. Profit% and loss% are always worked out on the Cost Price — never the selling price. The cost price is the shopkeeper's “starting money,” so it's the fair thing to measure the gain against. Burn this in: % is on CP.

Profit% = (Profit ÷ CP) × 100   and   Loss% = (Loss ÷ CP) × 100

✏️ Worked example — CP ₹400, SP ₹500 — find the profit%
  1. Profit = 500 − 400 = ₹100.
  2. Divide by the COST price (not 500!): 100 ÷ 400 = 0.25.
  3. Times 100: 0.25 × 100 = 25% profit. ✓
✏️ Worked example — CP ₹500, SP ₹400 — find the loss%
  1. Loss = 500 − 400 = ₹100.
  2. Divide by CP: 100 ÷ 500 = 0.20.
  3. Times 100: 20% loss. ✓
  4. Notice: the SAME ₹100 gap is 25% on a ₹400 cost but only 20% on a ₹500 cost — the cost price decides the percent.
Your turn. A pen costs ₹50 and is sold for ₹65. Find the profit%.
Show answer
Profit = 65 − 50 = ₹15. Profit% = 15 ÷ 50 × 100 = 30%.
⚠️ Common mistake

The most common profit% mistake: dividing the profit by the selling price instead of the cost. For CP ₹400, SP ₹500, profit ₹100, that wrongly gives 100 ÷ 500 = 20%. The right way: profit% is always on the cost price — 100 ÷ 400 = 25%. Why? Profit% measures the gain against the shopkeeper's starting money (what she paid), not against the price she ended at. Same numbers, but ₹100 is a bigger slice of ₹400 than of ₹500 — so the base you choose changes the answer. Always: % on CP.

Going the other way: find the selling price from a target profit%

Shopkeepers usually decide the profit% they want, then work out the selling price. To add a profit% onto the cost: find that percent of CP and add it on (exactly the percentage-increase move from Ch6). For a loss%, subtract instead.

✏️ Worked example — she wants 20% profit on a ₹200 item — what's the SP?
  1. Find 20% of the cost: 0.20 × 200 = ₹40 (the profit she wants).
  2. Add it to the cost: 200 + 40 = ₹240.
  3. Shortcut: 20% profit means SP is 120% of CP → 1.20 × 200 = ₹240. ✓
✏️ Worked example — a shirt costs ₹800 but is sold at a 10% loss — what's the SP?
  1. Find 10% of cost: 0.10 × 800 = ₹80 (the loss).
  2. Take it off the cost: 800 − 80 = ₹720.
  3. Shortcut: 10% loss means SP is 90% of CP → 0.90 × 800 = ₹720. ✓
Your turn. A baker’s cake costs ₹150 to make. She wants a 30% profit. What price should she sell it at?
Show answer
30% of 150 = ₹45 profit, so SP = 150 + 45 = ₹195.

Marked price & discount — the sale-tag maths

Walk into any shop and you'll see a Marked Price (MP) — the price printed on the tag (also called the list price). A discount is a cut off the marked price to tempt you to buy. And here's the partner to the golden rule:

🥝
⭐ Discount is always on the MARKED price (the tag), while profit/loss is always on the COST price. Don't mix them up! A “20% off” sticker means 20% off the tag — it says nothing about what the shop paid.

Discount = MP − SP,   and   Sale Price (SP) = MP − discount. The discount percent is on the marked price: Discount% = (Discount ÷ MP) × 100.

✏️ Worked example — a ₹500 jacket has “20% OFF” — what do you pay?
  1. Discount = 20% of the MARKED price: 0.20 × 500 = ₹100.
  2. Sale price = MP − discount: 500 − 100 = ₹400.
  3. Shortcut: paying after 20% off means paying 80% → 0.80 × 500 = ₹400. ✓
✏️ Worked example — a tag says MP ₹400, you pay ₹300 — what's the discount %?
  1. Discount = MP − SP = 400 − 300 = ₹100.
  2. Discount% on the MARKED price: 100 ÷ 400 × 100 = 25%. ✓
Your turn. A ₹250 toy is on “10% off”. What is the sale price?
Show answer
10% of 250 = ₹25 off, so you pay 250 − 25 = ₹225.

Putting it together: cost, tag, discount AND profit

Real shops do all of it at once. A clever shopkeeper buys cheaply (CP), marks the price up on the tag (MP), then offers a discount off the tag — and still makes a profit, because the sale price stays above her cost. The trick is to track which price each percentage is measured against.

✏️ Worked example — CP ₹400, marked at ₹500, with a 10% discount — does she profit?
  1. Discount is on the MARKED price: 10% of 500 = ₹50.
  2. Sale price = 500 − 50 = ₹450 (this is the SP).
  3. Now profit on the COST price: profit = 450 − 400 = ₹50.
  4. Profit% = 50 ÷ 400 × 100 = 12.5%. She gives ‘10% off’ AND keeps a 12.5% profit. 😎
🥝
See the magician's secret? ‘10% off ₹500’ sounds generous to you, but it's measured on the inflated tag, while her profit is measured on the real cost. Two different ‘wholes’, two different percentages — and she wins on both counts. Now you can read straight through any sale.
Your turn. CP ₹200, marked ₹300, 20% discount. Find the sale price and the profit.
Show answer
20% of 300 = ₹60 off → SP = 300 − 60 = ₹240. Profit = 240 − 200 = ₹40 (a 20% profit on the ₹200 cost).

  5. MP ₹800, you pay ₹680. How big was the discount in rupees?
    answer ₹120 (800 − 680).

Ratio & Proportion

Algebra · In Proportion Core
BIG QUESTIONTwo friends mix orange squash. Aarohi pours 2 cups of syrup into 6 cups of water; Ved pours 3 cups of syrup into 9 cups of water. Their cups are different sizes and the totals are different… yet the drinks taste exactly the same. How can two different mixes taste identical? By the end of this chapter you'll see why — and it's the secret behind every recipe, map and mixture on Earth.
🥝
Hi explorer, Kiwi here! 🥝 In Level 2 you shared things into fair, equal parts. A ratio is the grown-up of that idea: it compares two amounts by asking 'how many of these for each of those?' Ratios power cooking, mixing paint, reading maps and money. We'll build the whole idea from sharing — one careful step at a time.

A ratio compares two amounts

Suppose a fruit bowl has 3 mangoes and 4 apples. The ratio of mangoes to apples is written 3 : 4 and read 'three to four.' It does not say there are 7 fruits — it tells you the comparison: for every 3 mangoes there are 4 apples.

Order matters! 3 : 4 (mangoes to apples) is not the same as 4 : 3 (apples to mangoes). Always say the ratio in the order the words are given. The two numbers are called the terms of the ratio.

🥝
A ratio is a comparison, not a total. 3 : 4 could mean 3 and 4, or 6 and 8, or 30 and 40 — what stays fixed is the relationship: 'a bit fewer mangoes than apples, in 3-to-4 steps.'
Your turn. In a class there are 10 girls and 15 boys. Write the ratio of girls to boys, and then boys to girls.
Show answer
Girls : boys = 10 : 15. Boys : girls = 15 : 10. The order flips the numbers!

Equivalent ratios: many names for one comparison

Here's the squash surprise unwrapped. Look at Aarohi's mix 2 : 6 and Ved's mix 3 : 9. If you multiply or divide BOTH terms by the same number, the comparison doesn't change — you just get another name for it. These are equivalent ratios.

Why do they taste the same? Divide both of Aarohi's terms by 2 → 1 : 3. Divide both of Ved's by 3 → 1 : 3. Underneath, both drinks are 1 part syrup to 3 parts water. Same recipe, just scaled up or down.

✏️ Worked example — build three ratios equivalent to 2 : 3
  1. Multiply both terms by 2: 2×2 : 3×2 = 4 : 6.
  2. Multiply both terms by 5: 2×5 : 3×5 = 10 : 15.
  3. Multiply both terms by 10: 20 : 30.
  4. All of 2:3, 4:6, 10:15, 20:30 are the same comparison wearing different clothes.
🥝
Golden rule of ratios: do the same thing to BOTH terms — multiply both, or divide both, by the same number. (Never just one!) That keeps the comparison true.
Your turn. Are 4 : 5 and 12 : 15 equivalent?
Show answer
Multiply 4:5 by 3 → 12 : 15. Yes, they're equivalent. (Or check by cross-multiplying: 4 × 15 = 60 and 5 × 12 = 60 — equal, so they match.)

Simplest form: the smallest, neatest name

Just like fractions, every ratio has a simplest form — the smallest whole numbers that still give the same comparison. You reach it by dividing both terms by their biggest common factor (the HCF).

✏️ Worked example — write 18 : 24 in simplest form
  1. Find the biggest number that divides both 18 and 24. That's 6.
  2. Divide both terms by 6: 18 ÷ 6 = 3 and 24 ÷ 6 = 4.
  3. So 18 : 24 = 3 : 4 in simplest form. You can't go smaller — 3 and 4 share no common factor except 1.
Your turn. Write 40 : 50 in simplest form.
Show answer
The HCF of 40 and 50 is 10. Divide both by 10 → 4 : 5.
🥝
Simplest form is the ratio's 'home address.' To test whether two ratios are equal, simplify both — if they land on the same simplest form, they're equivalent.

Sharing in a ratio: the 'parts' trick

Now the most useful skill of all. Two friends share ₹35 in the ratio 3 : 4 (the older one did a little more work). How much does each get? The secret: count the parts.

✏️ Worked example — share ₹35 in the ratio 3 : 4
  1. Add the terms to get the total number of parts: 3 + 4 = 7 parts.
  2. Find the value of one part: ₹35 ÷ 7 = ₹5 per part.
  3. First share = 3 parts = 3 × ₹5 = ₹15. Second share = 4 parts = 4 × ₹5 = ₹20.
  4. Check: ₹15 + ₹20 = ₹35. ✓ And 15 : 20 simplifies back to 3 : 4. ✓
🥝
The recipe never changes: (1) add the ratio numbers to get total parts, (2) divide the quantity by total parts to get 'one part,' (3) multiply each ratio number by one part. Always check your two answers add back to the whole.
Your turn. Share 24 sweets between two children in the ratio 5 : 3.
Show answer
Total parts = 5 + 3 = 8. One part = 24 ÷ 8 = 3 sweets. Shares = 5×3 = 15 and 3×3 = 9. Check: 15 + 9 = 24. ✓

Proportion: when two ratios are equal

A proportion is simply a statement that two ratios are equal, like 2 : 5 = 8 : 20. We read it '2 is to 5 as 8 is to 20.' The squash mixes from the start were a proportion: 2 : 6 = 3 : 9.

There's a beautiful test. In a true proportion, the cross-products match: multiply the outer pair and the inner pair, and they're equal.

✏️ Worked example — is 2 : 5 = 8 : 20 a true proportion?
  1. Cross-multiply: outer × outer = 2 × 20 = 40.
  2. Inner × inner = 5 × 8 = 40.
  3. The cross-products are equal (40 = 40), so yes, it's a true proportion.

Cross-products do more than check — they let you find a missing term. Suppose a proportion has a gap: 3 : 4 = 9 : ?

✏️ Worked example — find the missing term in 3 : 4 = 9 : ?
  1. Notice 3 became 9 — that's ×3. Do the same to the other side: 4 × 3 = 12.
  2. So 3 : 4 = 9 : 12.
  3. Check with cross-products: 3 × 12 = 36 and 4 × 9 = 36. Equal! ✓
Your turn. Find the missing term: 2 : 7 = ? : 21.
Show answer
7 became 21, that's ×3, so 2 × 3 = 6. Thus 2 : 7 = 6 : 21. Check: 2 × 21 = 42 and 7 × 6 = 42. ✓

The unitary method: go through ONE

Here's a power-move that solves a huge family of problems: first find the value of one unit, then scale up to however many you want. It's called the unitary method — and it's secretly the 'one part' idea again.

✏️ Worked example — 5 identical pens cost ₹60. What do 8 pens cost?
  1. Find the cost of one pen: ₹60 ÷ 5 = ₹12 each.
  2. Now scale up to 8 pens: 8 × ₹12 = ₹96.
  3. Through-one thinking turned a tricky question into two easy steps.
🥝
Unitary method in a sentence: divide to reach one, then multiply to reach many. Whenever quantities scale together (more pens → more cost), this trick works.

It shines in recipes. A recipe uses 2 cups of flour for every 3 cups of sugar. You're doubling the cake and now have 9 cups of sugar — how much flour?

✏️ Worked example — flour needed for 9 cups of sugar (recipe is 2 flour : 3 sugar)
  1. Per one cup of sugar: flour = 2 ÷ 3 cup… but let's keep it whole by using parts.
  2. 9 cups of sugar is 9 ÷ 3 = 3 batches of the '3 sugar' unit.
  3. Each batch needs 2 cups of flour, so flour = 3 × 2 = 6 cups.
  4. Check the ratio: 6 flour : 9 sugar = 2 : 3. ✓ Same recipe, bigger cake.
Your turn. 4 apples cost ₹100. How much do 10 apples cost? (unitary method)
Show answer
One apple = ₹100 ÷ 4 = ₹25. Ten apples = 10 × ₹25 = ₹250.

Direct & Inverse Variation

Algebra · In Proportion Stretch
BIG QUESTIONOne painter takes 12 days to paint a long wall. So how long would 2 painters take? 6 days, you'd guess. And 4 painters? 3 days. More painters, fewer days. But buying more paint cans does the opposite — more cans, more cost. Two quantities, two completely different behaviours. Today we learn to tell them apart on sight — and meet a surprise: in maths, more is not always more.
🥝
Kiwi again! 🥝 You just mastered ratios. Now we watch what happens when one amount changes. Sometimes its partner rises with it; sometimes its partner falls. These two opposite stories are called direct variation and inverse variation, and almost every real-world 'rate' question is one of them. Let's meet them both.

Direct variation: they rise together

Pens cost ₹8 each. Buy more pens and you pay more — the cost grows in step with the number of pens. When two quantities go up together (and down together) so that their ratio stays the same, we say one varies directly with the other.

Here's the fingerprint of direct variation: cost ÷ pens is always the same. 8 ÷ 1 = 8, 24 ÷ 3 = 8, 48 ÷ 6 = 8. That fixed value (₹8 per pen) is called the constant — it never changes, and it's exactly the 'one part' / unitary value from Chapter 8.

✏️ Worked example — 4 books cost ₹200. What do 7 books cost?
  1. Spot the type: more books → more cost. That's direct variation.
  2. Find the constant (cost of one book): ₹200 ÷ 4 = ₹50 per book.
  3. Scale up to 7 books: 7 × ₹50 = ₹350.
  4. Check the ratio held: 200 ÷ 4 = 50 and 350 ÷ 7 = 50 — same constant. ✓
🥝
Test for direct variation: does the ratio (one ÷ other) stay constant? If yes, they vary directly — double one and the other doubles, halve one and the other halves.
Your turn. 2 kg of apples cost ₹90. What do 5 kg cost?
Show answer
Direct: more kg → more cost. One kg = ₹90 ÷ 2 = ₹45. Five kg = 5 × ₹45 = ₹225. (Check: 225 ÷ 5 = 45 = the constant.)

Inverse variation: as one rises, the other falls

Back to the wall. 4 painters finish in 6 days. Add painters and the job finishes sooner — one quantity goes up while the other goes down. When that happens so that their product stays the same, one quantity varies inversely with the other.

The fingerprint of inverse variation: painters × days is always the same. 4 × 6 = 24, 6 × 4 = 24, 8 × 3 = 24. That constant 24 is the total amount of work — call it '24 painter-days.' The wall always needs 24 painter-days; you can spread that work across many painters and few days, or few painters and many days.

✏️ Worked example — 6 workers dig a trench in 10 days. How long would 5 workers take?
  1. Spot the type: fewer workers → MORE days. That's inverse variation.
  2. Find the constant (total work): 6 × 10 = 60 worker-days.
  3. Now share 60 across 5 workers: 60 ÷ 5 = 12 days.
  4. Check: 5 × 12 = 60 — same product. ✓ Fewer hands, more time, same total job.
🥝
Test for inverse variation: does the product (one × other) stay constant? If yes, they vary inversely — double one and the other halves. Direct uses ÷ stays same; inverse uses × stays same. That one swap is the whole difference!
Your turn. A car covers a fixed road in 4 hours at 60 km/h. How long at 80 km/h?
Show answer
Faster speed → less time = inverse. Constant = distance = 60 × 4 = 240 km. Time at 80 = 240 ÷ 80 = 3 hours. (Check: 80 × 3 = 240.) ✓

The big question: which kind is it?

Before you compute anything, ask one question: when the first quantity goes UP, does the second go up or down? That single answer tells you the method.

• Goes up together (more → more) → direct → use the constant ratio (÷ stays same); find one, then multiply.
• Goes opposite (more → less) → inverse → use the constant product (× stays same); multiply to get the total, then divide.

🥝
Quick reflex drill — say 'same way' or 'opposite way':
• More petrol → more distance? same way → direct.
• More taps filling a tank → less time? opposite → inverse.
• More speed → less time for a trip? opposite → inverse.
• More hours worked → more pay? same way → direct.
✏️ Worked example — decide and solve: 3 pipes fill a tank in 8 hours; how long with 4 pipes?
  1. More pipes → less time. Opposite way, so inverse variation.
  2. Constant product = 3 × 8 = 24 pipe-hours.
  3. With 4 pipes: 24 ÷ 4 = 6 hours.
  4. Check: 4 × 6 = 24. ✓
Your turn. Decide the type, then solve: 3 metres of cloth cost ₹150. What do 8 metres cost?
Show answer
More cloth → more cost = direct. One metre = 150 ÷ 3 = ₹50. Eight metres = 8 × 50 = ₹400.

Surprise: 'more is not always more'

Most people's gut feeling is 'more always means more.' Inverse variation proves that wrong in everyday life — and it can save you from real mistakes. Picture food on a long trek.

✏️ Worked example — food lasts 12 hikers for 20 days. How long will it last 16 hikers?
  1. More mouths → food runs out SOONER. Opposite way → inverse variation.
  2. Constant = total food = 12 × 20 = 240 person-days of food.
  3. Share among 16 people: 240 ÷ 16 = 15 days.
  4. Surprise check: more people (16 > 12) gave fewer days (15 < 20). 'More' made the answer smaller — exactly because it's inverse!
🥝
Whenever a problem feels like 'more should give more' but the answer comes out smaller — pause. You've spotted inverse variation. Trust the product stays constant, not your gut. (And if you ever multiply when you should divide, your answer will be wildly too big — a free mistake-detector!)

Vedic Speed Maths

Arithmetic · Speed Magic Stretch
BIG QUESTIONPick any two-digit number and multiply it by 11 — in your head, in one second, before a calculator could even switch on. Impossible? By the end of this page you'll do it. Ta-da! 🎩✨ Today I'm Kiwi the Math Magician. Long ago in India, mathematicians found lightning-fast ways to calculate — collected into a system people call Vedic Maths. 🔮 Trick 1 — Times eleven, in one second

Watch this magic: 35 × 11. I split the digits 3 and 5 apart, add them to get the middle, and… 385. Done.

✏️ Worked example — 35 × 11
  1. Pull the two digits apart and leave a gap:  3  _  5.
  2. Add the two digits and drop the answer into the gap: 3 + 5 = 8.
  3. Read it straight off: 3 8 5 = 385. (Check: 35 × 11 = 385. ✓)

But what if the middle sum is 10 or more? Then we just carry, exactly like normal adding. Try 76 × 11:

✏️ Worked example — 76 × 11 (with a carry)
  1. Outer digits: 7 _ 6.
  2. Middle: 7 + 6 = 13 — that's two digits, too big for one slot.
  3. Keep the 3, carry the 1 left onto the 7: 7 + 1 = 8.
  4. Read it: 8 3 6 = 836. (Check: 76 × 11 = 836. ✓)
🥝

🔮 Trick 7 — The 9-check: catch a wrong answer red-handed

Here's the most useful magic of all — a mistake detector. Take any number and keep adding its digits until just one digit is left (this one-digit squeeze is called its digit sum). For 1161: 1+1+6+1 = 9. For 27: 2+7 = 9.

The surprise: when you do a multiplication, the digit sums obey the same multiplication. Test 27 × 43 = 1161: digit-sum of 27 is 9, of 43 is 7. Now 9 × 7 = 63, and its digit sum is 6 + 3 = 9. The answer 1161 also squeezes to 9. They match — so the answer passes the check.

Now watch it catch a liar. Suppose someone claims 68 × 47 = 3186:

✏️ Worked example — the 9-check catches an error
  1. Left side check: digit sum of 68 = 6+8 = 14 → 1+4 = 5; of 47 = 4+7 = 11 → 2.
  2. Multiply the checks: 5 × 2 = 10 → 1+0 = 1. So a correct answer MUST squeeze to 1.
  3. Their answer 3186 squeezes to 3+1+8+6 = 18 → 9.
  4. 9 ≠ 1 — caught! The real answer is 68 × 47 = 3196 (which squeezes to 3+1+9+6 = 19 → 1 ✓).

It checks addition as well. For 4825 + 1376: digit sums 1 and 8 → 1 + 8 = 9; the true total 6201 squeezes to 9 ✓. A claimed total of 6101 would squeeze to 8 — instantly wrong.

🥝
🔑 The secret. Squeezing the digits is really finding the remainder after dividing by 9 (mathematicians call it “casting out nines”). Remainders survive multiplication and addition, so the check digit of the answer must equal the check digit you get from the parts. ⚠️ Honest warning: it can miss some errors (if a mistake is an exact multiple of 9, or two digits get swapped, the check digit doesn't change). It never gives a false alarm though — if the check fails, the answer is definitely wrong. A free 5-second safety net for every calculation you do.

Sequences and their terms

A sequence is just a list of numbers in a set order, made by a rule. Each number is a term. We talk about the 1st term, 2nd term, 3rd term, and so on. Here's the sequence of even numbers, with the rule '+2 each time':

Arithmetic sequences: the same jump every time

The friendliest sequences add the same number at every step. That fixed jump is called the common difference, and such a list is an arithmetic sequence. Watch 3, 7, 11, 15, …

✏️ Worked example — find the common difference and the next two terms of 5, 12, 19, 26, …
  1. Find the jump: 12 − 5 = 7, and 19 − 12 = 7. The common difference is +7.
  2. Next term: 26 + 7 = 33.
  3. And the one after: 33 + 7 = 40. So it continues 5, 12, 19, 26, 33, 40.

Jumping ahead: the nth term

Here's the real power. Suppose you want the 20th term of 2, 5, 8, 11, … Listing all 20 is slow. Instead, notice you start at 2 and then add the common difference one fewer time than the position — because the 1st term hasn't jumped yet.

✏️ Worked example — find the 10th term of 2, 5, 8, 11, …
  1. First term = 2; common difference = 3.
  2. To reach the 10th term you add the jump (10 − 1) = 9 times: 9 × 3 = 27.
  3. 10th term = first term + 27 = 2 + 27 = 29.
  4. Rule in words: nth term = first term + (n − 1) × common difference. No listing needed!
🥝
The nth-term rule is your teleporter: start value + (position − 1) × jump. It lets you land on the 100th term as easily as the 3rd. We use (n − 1) because the very first term hasn't jumped yet.

Square numbers and a hidden surprise

Now arrange dots in full square grids: 1×1, 2×2, 3×3, … The counts 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, … are the square numbers (the nth one is simply n × n).

✏️ Worked example — add the odd numbers: 1, 1+3, 1+3+5, 1+3+5+7

  2. 1 + 3 = 4 = 2².

  4. 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 = 4².

Gauss's lightning trick: summing 1 + 2 + … + n

Back to the big question. To add 1 + 2 + 3 + … + 100, Gauss paired the first with the last: 1 + 100 = 101. Then 2 + 99 = 101. Then 3 + 98 = 101… every pair makes 101!

There are 100 numbers, so they form 50 pairs, each adding to 101. The grand total is 50 × 101 = 5050. No long addition — just one clever pairing.

✏️ Worked example — add 1 + 2 + 3 + … + 10 the Gauss way
  1. Pair first + last: 1 + 10 = 11. Next pair: 2 + 9 = 11. Then 3 + 8 = 11, 4 + 7 = 11, 5 + 6 = 11.
  2. That's 5 pairs, each summing to 11.
  3. Total = 5 × 11 = 55. (And 55 is exactly the 10th triangular number — the same idea!)
  4. General rule: the sum 1 + 2 + … + n = n × (n + 1) ÷ 2. Check n = 10: 10 × 11 ÷ 2 = 55. ✓
🥝

    answer First 5 odd numbers, so the sum is 5² = 25.

The Language of Algebra

Algebra · Letter Maths Core
BIG QUESTIONI'm thinking of a number. I double it, add 5, and get 17. What was my number? You could guess… or you could write the whole riddle as one short line — 2x + 5 = 17 — and solve it like unlocking a safe. That short line is algebra: the language that lets you do maths with numbers you don't know yet.
🥝
Kiwi here! 🥝 In Level 2 you let a letter stand for a 'mystery number' and solved simple balances. Now we turn that into a real language — with its own grammar: variables, terms, coefficients, and rules for tidying expressions and solving equations. Don't worry, every new word names something you already understand. Let's build the language piece by piece.

Variables: letters that hold a number

A variable is a letter that stands for a number we don't know yet, or one that can change. We usually use x, y, n or a. A constant is a fixed number that never changes, like 5 or 12. Think of a variable as a sealed box and a constant as a price tag you can already read.

🥝
Two quick words: a variable wears a letter and can change; a constant is a plain number and stays put. In n + 7: variable = n, constant = 7.
Your turn. In the expression b + 12, name the variable and the constant.
Show answer
Variable = b (the letter — our unknown). Constant = 12 (a fixed number).

Inside an expression: terms and coefficients

An expression is a short math phrase with no equals sign, like 3x + 5. The pieces joined by + or − signs are the terms — here 3x and 5. In the term 3x, the number in front, 3, is the coefficient — it tells you how many x's there are, so 3x means x + x + x. The lonely number 5, with no letter, is the constant term.

✏️ Worked example — name every part of 3x + 5
  1. Terms (joined by +): 3x and 5.
  2. Variable: x.
  3. Coefficient: 3 (how many x's).
  4. Constant term: 5 (a fixed number, no letter).
🥝
In algebra we drop the times sign: 3x means 3 × x. A number snuggled next to a letter always means multiply. And a lonely x secretly has coefficient 1, because x = 1x.
Your turn. In 7y + 3, name the coefficient and the constant term.
Show answer
Coefficient = 7; constant term = 3.

Like terms: tidying an expression

Terms that use the same letter are like terms, and they can be combined — just add or subtract their coefficients. Terms with different letters, or a letter versus a plain number, are unlike and must stay apart. (You can add 3 apples + 2 apples = 5 apples, but you can't add 3 apples + 2 oranges into one kind of fruit.)

✏️ Worked example — simplify 4x + 3 + 2x + 5
  1. Group the like terms. The x-terms: 4x and 2x. The constants: 3 and 5.
  2. Add the x-terms: 4x + 2x = 6x.
  3. Add the constants: 3 + 5 = 8.
  4. Put it back together: 6x + 8. We can't combine 6x with 8 — different kinds.
🥝
Rule for simplifying: collect like with like. Add the coefficients of matching letters; add the plain numbers. Never mix an x-term with a number — they're different 'fruits.'
Your turn. Simplify 7a + 2a − a.
Show answer
All like terms (all 'a'): 7 + 2 − 1 = 8, so the answer is 8a. (Remember the lonely a counts as 1a.)

Turning words into expressions

The magic of algebra is writing a whole sentence as one short expression. Let a letter stand for the unknown, then translate the words into + − × or ÷.

'6 more than a number' → n + 6.
'double a number' → 2n.
'5 less than a number' → n − 5.
'a number shared into 4 equal parts' → n ÷ 4.
'double a number, then add 3' → 2n + 3.

✏️ Worked example — write an expression: 'Ved has some coins, then finds 4 times as many more'
  1. Let Ved's coins be c (the unknown).
  2. '4 times as many more' adds 4c to what he had.
  3. So the total is c + 4c = 5c. (Like terms combine!) If later c = 3, he has 5 × 3 = 15 coins.
Your turn. Write an expression for: 'A number of apples, tripled, then 2 taken away.' Use the letter a.
Show answer
Triple the apples: 3a. Then take 2 away: 3a − 2.

Opening the box: evaluating by substitution

When someone tells us the value of the variable, we substitute — swap the letter for its number — and work it out. Watch out for the order: do the multiplying first, then the adding.

✏️ Worked example — evaluate 3x + 5 when x = 4
  1. Swap x for 4: the term 3x becomes 3 × 4 = 12.
  2. Now add the constant: 12 + 5 = 17.
  3. So when x = 4, the expression 3x + 5 equals 17. (That's our opening riddle, almost solved!)
🥝
Substitution = 'open the box and put the real number in.' Always multiply the letter-terms before adding the constant — same order-of-operations rule you already know.
Your turn. Evaluate 2n + 7 when n = 6.
Show answer
2n = 2 × 6 = 12, then 12 + 7 = 19.
Your turn. Evaluate 20 − 3y when y = 4.
Show answer
3y = 3 × 4 = 12, then 20 − 12 = 8.

Equations: keep the balance level

Put an equals sign between two things and you get an equation — a full sentence saying 'the left side weighs the same as the right.' Picture a balance scale: equal pans stay perfectly level.

Golden rule of the scale: whatever you do to one pan, do to the other, or it tips. To free the box, take 3 off the left — so take 3 off the right too:

✏️ Worked example — solve x + 3 = 7
  1. The left pan has an extra +3. Remove 3 from the left.
  2. To stay balanced, remove 3 from the right as well: 7 − 3 = 4.
  3. Now the left is just x and the right is 4, so x = 4.
  4. Check: 4 + 3 = 7. ✓

Subtraction undoes with addition; multiplication undoes with division. 3x = 12 means 'three boxes weigh 12,' so one box is 12 shared into 3:

✏️ Worked example — solve 3x = 12
  1. Three identical boxes weigh 12 together. Share equally: divide both sides by 3.
  2. 12 ÷ 3 = 4, so one box weighs 4 → x = 4.
  3. Check: 3 × 4 = 12. ✓
Your turn. Solve x − 4 = 9 using the balance rule.
Show answer
Add 4 to both sides: 9 + 4 = 13, so x = 13. Check: 13 − 4 = 9. ✓
Your turn. Solve x ÷ 5 = 4.
Show answer
Multiply both sides by 5: 4 × 5 = 20, so x = 20. Check: 20 ÷ 5 = 4. ✓

Two-step equations: undo in reverse order

Now we solve our opening riddle, 2x + 5 = 17. It was built by first multiplying by 2, then adding 5. To unlock it, we undo in the reverse order: peel off the +5 first, then undo the ×2 — like taking off your shoes before your socks.

✏️ Worked example — solve 2x + 5 = 17
  1. Undo the +5: subtract 5 from both sides. 17 − 5 = 12, leaving 2x = 12.
  2. Undo the ×2: divide both sides by 2. 12 ÷ 2 = 6, so x = 6.
  3. Check: 2 × 6 + 5 = 12 + 5 = 17. ✓ Riddle solved — my number was 6!
🥝
Two-step recipe: first undo the + or − (move the constant), then undo the × or ÷ (the coefficient). Always do the same to both sides, and always check by putting your answer back in.
✏️ Worked example — solve 3x − 5 = 16
  1. Undo the −5: add 5 to both sides. 16 + 5 = 21, so 3x = 21.
  2. Undo the ×3: divide both sides by 3. 21 ÷ 3 = 7, so x = 7.
  3. Check: 3 × 7 − 5 = 21 − 5 = 16. ✓
Your turn. Solve 5x + 2 = 17.
Show answer
Subtract 2: 5x = 15. Divide by 5: x = 3. Check: 5 × 3 + 2 = 17. ✓
Your turn. Solve 4x − 7 = 13.
Show answer
Add 7: 4x = 20. Divide by 4: x = 5. Check: 4 × 5 − 7 = 13. ✓

Now climb the ladder

Read each phrase or equation carefully — name the parts, simplify, substitute, or solve. Peek only after you try!

    answer 5.
  2. In 5x + 9, what is the constant term?

  4. Are 3x and 5x like terms or unlike terms?

    answer Add coefficients: 6 + 2 = 8 → 8x.
  2. Simplify 9k − 4k.
    answer 9 − 4 = 5 → 5k.

    answer n + 7.

    answer 2x + 5 = 17 → subtract 5: 2x = 12 → divide by 2: x = 6.
  3. Invent a 'collect like terms' expression that simplifies to 5y + 4, and show the steps.
    answer e.g. 2y + 1 + 3y + 3 → (2y + 3y) + (1 + 3) = 5y + 4. Any matching split works.

Lines & Angles

Geometry · Angle Chasers Core
BIG QUESTIONTwo straight roads cross. A signpost tells you just one of the four angles they make is 50°. Without a protractor — without measuring anything — can you find the other three? By the end of this chapter you'll do it in your head, and feel like a detective who reads angles the way you read words.
🥝
Hi explorer, it's Kiwi! 🧭 In Level 2 you flipped and turned shapes. Now we zoom right in on the corners — the angles. An angle is just the amount of turn between two lines that meet. We'll learn to name angles, measure them, and best of all — to chase an unknown angle using rules instead of a ruler. That's the superpower of this whole part of the book.

An angle is an amount of turn

Picture the hands of a clock, or a door swinging open. When two straight lines (we call them rays) share a starting point — the vertex — the gap between them is the angle. We measure that turn in degrees (°). A full spin all the way around is 360°; a quarter-turn is 90°.

Why 360 for a full turn? It's an ancient choice (the Babylonians liked 360 because it divides so neatly), and it stuck because 360 splits cleanly into halves, thirds, quarters, sixths and more. Keep that in your pocket: full turn = 360°, half turn = 180°, quarter turn = 90°.

The five kinds of angles

Angles get names based on their size. Meet the whole family:

🥝
Quick check, no measuring needed — just compare to 90° and 180°:
• smaller than 90° → acute
• exactly 90° → right
• between 90° and 180° → obtuse
• exactly 180° → straight
• between 180° and 360° → reflex
Your turn. Sort these by name: 12°, 90°, 175°, 200°, 180°.
Show answer
12° acute · 90° right · 175° obtuse · 200° reflex · 180° straight.

Measuring with a protractor

A protractor is a half-circle ruler marked from 0° to 180°. To measure an angle:

✏️ Worked example — measure an angle with a protractor
  1. Place the protractor's centre dot exactly on the vertex (the corner).
  2. Turn it so one ray runs along the 0° line (the baseline).
  3. Read where the other ray crosses the scale. Use the scale that starts at 0° on your baseline — a protractor has two scales so you don't read the wrong one!
  4. Here the second ray points to 60°, so the angle is 60°.
🥝
The two-scale trap is the #1 mistake. Easy fix: first guess whether the angle is acute or obtuse. If it looks acute, your answer must be under 90°. If your reading says 120° but the angle is clearly a tiny acute one, you read the wrong scale — flip to the other.
Your turn. Your ray starts on the 0° baseline and the other crosses the scale at the 110° mark. Is the angle acute or obtuse, and what is its size?
Show answer
110° is between 90° and 180°, so it's obtuse, measuring 110°.

Two angles that team up: complementary & supplementary

Some angle pairs add to special totals — and that's where angle-chasing begins.

Complementary angles add up to 90° (think: they make a right angle together). Supplementary angles add up to 180° (they make a straight line together).

✏️ Worked example — find the missing partner
  1. An angle is 35°. Its complement = 90 − 35 = 55°.
  2. The same 35° angle's supplement = 180 − 35 = 145°.
  3. No guessing, no measuring — just subtract from 90 or from 180!
🥝
A memory hook some students love: C comes before S in the alphabet, and 90 comes before 180. So Complementary → 90°, Supplementary → 180°.
Your turn. Two angles are supplementary. One is 72°. What is the other?
Show answer
180 − 72 = 108°.
Your turn. An angle and its complement are equal. What is each angle?
Show answer
If both are equal and add to 90°, each is 90 ÷ 2 = 45°.

Angles on a straight line add to 180°

Here is the first big chasing rule. If several angles sit in a row along one side of a straight line, they always add up to 180° — because a straight line is a half-turn.

✏️ Worked example — chase the unknown on a line
  1. The three angles share a straight line, so they total 180°.
  2. Known so far: 50° + 70° = 120°.
  3. Missing angle = 180 − 120 = 60°. Detective work, not a ruler!
Your turn. On a straight line, two angles are 90° and x. Find x.
Show answer
x = 180 − 90 = 90°. (Two right angles make a straight line.)

Angles around a point add to 360°

Go all the way around a single point and you've made a full turn. So all the angles meeting at a point must total 360°.

✏️ Worked example — chase the unknown around a point
  1. Angles around the point add to 360°.
  2. Known: 90 + 120 + 60 = 270°.
  3. Missing angle = 360 − 270 = 90°.
Your turn. Three equal angles meet at a point. What is each one?
Show answer
360 ÷ 3 = 120° each.

The crossing surprise: vertically opposite angles

When two straight lines cross, they make four angles. The two angles that are directly across from each other (sharing only the vertex) are called vertically opposite — and here's the surprise: they are always equal.

Why must they be equal? Watch the chase: a + b = 180° (they sit on a straight line), and the angle next to b on the other side also makes 180° with b. Two things that both fill the gap left by b must be the same size. So the opposite angles match — proved, not measured!

✏️ Worked example — one angle unlocks all four
  1. Two roads cross; one angle is 50°.
  2. Its vertically opposite angle is also 50°.
  3. Each of the other two angles is supplementary to 50°: 180 − 50 = 130°.
  4. So the four angles are 50°, 130°, 50°, 130° — and they add to 360° ✓. There's the answer to our Big Question!
Your turn. Two lines cross. One angle is 110°. Give all four angles.
Show answer
110°, and its opposite 110°; the other pair are 180 − 110 = 70° each. So 110°, 70°, 110°, 70°.

Parallel lines cut by a transversal

Parallel lines are two straight lines that run in the same direction and never meet — like rails of a train track. We mark them with little arrows. Now draw a third line crossing both of them; that crossing line is a transversal.

Because the parallel lines have the same slant, the transversal makes the same set of angles at each crossing. Two beautiful patterns pop out:

• Corresponding angles (matching position at each crossing — like x and y in the figure, both in the 'top-left' spot) are equal.
• Alternate angles (on opposite sides of the transversal, tucked between the parallels) are also equal.

🥝
Picture sliding the top crossing straight down the transversal until it lands on the bottom crossing. Because the lines are parallel, everything lines up perfectly — that's the real reason corresponding angles match. Alternate angles then follow, because they're vertically opposite to corresponding ones.
✏️ Worked example — chase across parallel lines
  1. The transversal makes a 70° angle with the top parallel line.
  2. The corresponding angle at the bottom line is also 70°.
  3. The angle next to it (on the straight bottom line) is its supplement: 180 − 70 = 110°.
  4. So once you know one angle in a parallel-lines picture, you can fill in every angle as 70° or 110°. ✨
Your turn. A transversal crosses two parallel lines. One angle is 65°. Its alternate angle (between the parallels, other side) is what?
Show answer
Alternate angles are equal, so it's also 65°.

Triangles & Polygons

Geometry · Angle Chasers Core
BIG QUESTIONDraw any triangle you like — long and skinny, fat and squat, tilted any way. Measure its three corner angles and add them up. Try a totally different triangle and add again. Something almost magical happens: you keep getting the same number. What is it, and why can it never change?
🥝
Welcome back, it's Kiwi! 🔺 In the last chapter you learned to chase angles on lines. Now we close those lines into shapes — starting with the triangle, the simplest and strongest shape of all. We'll discover a rule that every triangle in the universe obeys, then ride it all the way up to many-sided polygons.

Naming triangles — by sides and by angles

A triangle is a closed shape with three straight sides and three corners (vertices). We can name a triangle two ways:

By its sides:
• Equilateral — all three sides equal.
• Isosceles — exactly two sides equal.
• Scalene — all three sides different.

By its angles:
• Acute triangle — all three angles less than 90°.
• Right triangle — one angle exactly 90°.
• Obtuse triangle — one angle more than 90°.

🥝
A neat fact to remember: in an equilateral triangle, all three angles are equal too — and since they must add to 180° (you'll prove that next!), each one is exactly 180 ÷ 3 = 60°. In an isosceles triangle, the two angles opposite the equal sides are also equal — a super-useful shortcut.
Your turn. A triangle has sides 6 cm, 6 cm, 9 cm. What type is it (by sides)?
Show answer
Two sides are equal, the third is different → isosceles.

The big discovery: a triangle's angles add to 180°

Here's a hands-on way to see it. Tear the three corners off a paper triangle and lay them side by side, point to point. They snap together to make a perfectly straight line — and a straight line is 180°!

That's not a coincidence for one lucky triangle — it's true for every triangle. Mathematicians call it the Angle Sum Property:

🥝
In every triangle, the three interior angles add up to exactly 180°. This one fact is the master key to all triangle problems. Know two angles → you instantly know the third.
✏️ Worked example — find the missing angle of a triangle
  1. Two angles of a triangle are 80° and 60°.
  2. All three add to 180°, so the third = 180 − 80 − 60.
  3. Third angle = 180 − 140 = 40°. Check: 80 + 60 + 40 = 180 ✓.
Your turn. A right triangle has one angle of 35° (besides its 90° corner). Find the third angle.
Show answer
180 − 90 − 35 = 55°.
Your turn. Each angle of an equilateral triangle is the same. What is each one?
Show answer
180 ÷ 3 = 60°.

A bonus rule: the exterior angle

Extend one side of a triangle past a corner. The angle that opens up outside the triangle is the exterior angle. Surprise: it equals the sum of the two far interior angles (the two it does not touch).

Why? The exterior angle and the interior angle next to it sit on a straight line, so they add to 180°. And the three interior angles also add to 180°. Subtract the shared interior angle from both and — out pops the rule: exterior angle = sum of the other two interior angles.

✏️ Worked example — use the exterior-angle rule
  1. Two far interior angles are 70° and 60°.
  2. Exterior angle = 70 + 60 = 130°.
  3. Double-check: the interior angle at that corner = 180 − 130 = 50°, and 70 + 60 + 50 = 180 ✓.
Your turn. A triangle's two remote interior angles are 45° and 65°. What is the exterior angle at the third vertex?
Show answer
45 + 65 = 110°.

From triangles to quadrilaterals: the angles add to 360°

A quadrilateral is a four-sided shape (square, rectangle, kite, any four-corner shape). Here's a slick trick: draw one diagonal and it splits the quadrilateral into two triangles.

✏️ Worked example — why a quadrilateral's angles total 360°
  1. The diagonal makes 2 triangles.
  2. Each triangle's angles add to 180°.
  3. Together: 2 × 180 = 360°. So every quadrilateral's four angles add to 360°!
Your turn. Three angles of a quadrilateral are 100°, 80° and 95°. Find the fourth.
Show answer
360 − 100 − 80 − 95 = 85°.

That diagonal trick keeps working! From one corner of any polygon, draw diagonals to every other corner. A shape with n sides always splits into (n − 2) triangles. Multiply by 180° and you have the total of all its interior angles:

Interior-angle sum of an n-sided polygon = (n − 2) × 180°.

Regular polygons — every angle the same

A regular polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal (like a stop sign, which is a regular octagon). Since every angle is the same, just share the total fairly:

Each interior angle of a regular n-gon = (n − 2) × 180° ÷ n.

✏️ Worked example — each angle of a regular hexagon
  1. Total interior angles = (6 − 2) × 180 = 720°.
  2. Share equally among 6 corners: 720 ÷ 6 = 120° each.
  3. That's why honeycomb cells (regular hexagons) tile so perfectly — three 120° angles meet at a point and 3 × 120 = 360° fills the gap exactly. 🐝
🥝

For a regular n-gon, each exterior angle = 360 ÷ n, and each interior angle = 180 − that. For a hexagon: 360 ÷ 6 = 60° exterior → 180 − 60 = 120° interior. Same answer! ✓

Perimeter, Area & Circles

Geometry · Space & Surface Core
BIG QUESTIONYou have 20 metres of fence. What shape of garden gives you the most growing space inside? A long thin strip? A square? Could a circle beat them all? Hidden in this question is the difference between perimeter (the fence) and area (the space) — and a number so special it has its own symbol: π.
🥝
Hello again, it's Kiwi! 🟠 Two different questions live in every flat shape: how far around? (perimeter) and how much surface inside? (area). We measure perimeter in length units like cm, and area in square units like cm². Let's build every area formula from one simple idea — counting squares — and finish with the magic of circles.

Area by counting squares: rectangle & square

Area is the number of unit squares that fit inside a shape. Look at this rectangle covered in 1-cm squares:

Counting one by one works, but notice: 3 rows of 5 squares is just 5 × 3. That's the shortcut for every rectangle.

✏️ Worked example — rectangle area & perimeter
  1. Rectangle: length 5 cm, width 3 cm.
  2. Area = length × width = 5 × 3 = 15 cm² (square units!).
  3. Perimeter = 2 × (length + width) = 2 × (5 + 3) = 16 cm (length units).
  4. Same shape, two very different measurements — never mix them up!

A square is a rectangle whose sides are all equal, so its area is side × side:

✏️ Worked example — square area & perimeter (side 6 cm)
  1. Area = side × side = 6 × 6 = 36 cm².
  2. Perimeter = 4 × side = 4 × 6 = 24 cm.
Your turn. A rectangle is 9 m long and 4 m wide. Find its area and perimeter.
Show answer
Area = 9 × 4 = 36 m². Perimeter = 2 × (9 + 4) = 2 × 13 = 26 m.

Area of a triangle — half of a rectangle

Here's a lovely surprise. Take any triangle, copy it, flip the copy, and the two together make a parallelogram (and a right triangle makes a perfect rectangle). So a triangle is exactly half of that box around it:

Area of a triangle = ½ × base × height. The height must be measured straight (perpendicular) from the base to the opposite tip — that's the dashed line.

✏️ Worked example — triangle area (base 8, height 5)
  1. Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × 8 × 5.
  2. ½ × 8 = 4, then 4 × 5 = 20 cm².
  3. Sense-check: the surrounding 8 × 5 = 40 cm² rectangle is twice as big — and 20 is half of 40 ✓.
🥝
Watch out: the height is not always a side! For a slanted (obtuse) triangle, the perpendicular height might even fall outside the triangle. Always use the straight, perpendicular distance from the base to the far corner.
Your turn. A triangular sail has base 6 m and height 9 m. Find its area.
Show answer
½ × 6 × 9 = ½ × 54 = 27 m².

Area of a parallelogram — straighten it into a rectangle

A parallelogram looks like a pushed-over rectangle. Slice a triangle off one slanted end and slide it to the other end — it becomes a plain rectangle of the same base and height. So:

Area of a parallelogram = base × height (height measured perpendicular to the base, not the slanted side).

✏️ Worked example — parallelogram area (base 10, height 6)
  1. Area = base × height = 10 × 6 = 60 cm².
  2. The slanted side length doesn't matter for area — only the straight-up height does.
Your turn. A parallelogram has base 12 cm and height 5 cm. Find its area.
Show answer
12 × 5 = 60 cm².

Composite shapes — cut into friendly pieces

Real shapes (like an L-shaped room) aren't always one neat rectangle. The trick: chop the shape into rectangles, find each area, and add them up.

✏️ Worked example — area of the L-shape
  1. Split into a tall left rectangle and a short right rectangle.
  2. Left rectangle: 5 cm wide × 7 cm tall = 35 cm². (Top arm width 5, full height 7.)
  3. Right rectangle: 8 cm wide × 4 cm tall = 32 cm². (Bottom foot.)
  4. Total area = 35 + 32 = 67 cm². Add the pieces — never count any square twice!
🥝
Two ways to handle composites: add the pieces (split into rectangles) or subtract — find the area of a big enclosing rectangle and take away the missing corner. Both give the same answer; pick whichever is easier for the picture.
Your turn. A 10 cm × 6 cm rectangle has a 3 cm × 2 cm bite cut out of one corner. What area is left?
Show answer
Whole = 10 × 6 = 60 cm²; bite = 3 × 2 = 6 cm²; left = 60 − 6 = 54 cm².

Meet the circle — radius, diameter, circumference

A circle is the set of all points the same distance from a centre. Three words you need:

• Radius (r): centre to edge.
• Diameter (d): all the way across through the centre — exactly twice the radius, so d = 2r.
• Circumference (C): the distance around the circle (its 'perimeter').

Your turn. A circle has radius 7 cm. What is its diameter?
Show answer
d = 2r = 2 × 7 = 14 cm.

The surprise of all surprises: π

Here's an experiment people have done for thousands of years. Take any circle — a coin, a plate, a wheel — and measure two things: the distance around it (C) and the distance across it (d). Now divide C by d. You always get the same number, a little more than 3:

That never-changing ratio C ÷ d is the famous number π ("pi"). π ≈ 3.14 ≈ 22/7. It goes on forever without repeating (3.14159265…), but for school we use 3.14 or 22/7.

🥝
Read that again — it's astonishing. It doesn't matter if the circle is a tiny button or as big as a planet: go around it and across it, divide, and you always get π. The circle carries this secret number baked right into its shape. From C ÷ d = π we get the circumference formula:

Circumference C = π × d = 2 × π × r.

✏️ Worked example — circumference of a wheel (radius 7 cm)
  1. Use π ≈ 22/7 because the radius is 7 (the 7s cancel beautifully).
  2. C = 2 × π × r = 2 × (22/7) × 7.
  3. The 7 in the bottom cancels the 7 on top: 2 × 22 = 44 cm.
Your turn. A circular pond has diameter 10 m. Find its circumference (use π ≈ 3.14).
Show answer
C = π × d = 3.14 × 10 = 31.4 m.

Area of a circle = π r²

There's a clever way to see this. Slice a circle into many thin wedges and re-arrange them, tips pointing up and down alternately. They form an almost-rectangle: its long side is half the circumference (π r) and its short side is the radius (r). So the area is π r × r = π r².

Area of a circle = π × r × r = π r². (Remember: use the radius, and square it.)

✏️ Worked example — area of a circle (radius 7 cm)
  1. Area = π × r² = (22/7) × 7 × 7.
  2. (22/7) × 7 = 22, then 22 × 7 = 154 cm².
  3. Careful: r² means r × r = 49, not 14. Square the radius, then multiply by π.
🥝
Don't confuse the two circle formulas! Circumference uses r once (C = 2πr) and is a length (cm). Area uses r twice (A = πr²) and is a surface (cm²). The little 'squared' tells you it's area.
Your turn. Find the area of a circle with radius 10 cm (use π ≈ 3.14).
Show answer
A = π r² = 3.14 × 10 × 10 = 3.14 × 100 = 314 cm².

BIG QUESTIONPick up a dice, a cardboard box, a can, a party hat. Count their flat faces, their edges (where two faces meet) and their corners (vertices). • Face — a flat surface.

Nets — solids unfolded flat

A net is what you get when you carefully unfold a solid and lay it flat — like flattening a cardboard box. Fold it back up and you rebuild the solid. A cube unfolds into six squares, but not every arrangement of six squares folds into a cube!

🥝
There are exactly 11 different nets that fold into a cube — a fun thing to hunt for! Quick test: when you mentally fold it, every square must become a separate side with no overlaps and no gaps. If two squares would land on the same face, it's not a valid net.
Your turn. A cube has 6 faces. How many squares must its net contain?
Show answer
Exactly 6 — one for each face.

The secret of dice: opposite faces add to 7

A standard dice (die) is a cube with 1 to 6 pips. Its hidden rule: every pair of opposite faces adds up to 7. So 1 is opposite 6, 2 is opposite 5, and 3 is opposite 4.

✏️ Worked example — if the top shows 2, what's on the bottom?
  1. Opposite faces add to 7.
  2. Bottom = 7 − top = 7 − 2 = 5.
  3. Bonus: the three faces you can see plus their three hidden opposites always total 3 × 7 = 21 — that's every number from 1 to 6 added up!
Your turn. A die rests with 4 on top. What number is face-down on the table?
Show answer
7 − 4 = 3.

Line symmetry — the fold-in-half test

A shape has line symmetry (also called reflection symmetry) if you can fold it along a straight line so the two halves match exactly. That fold line is a line of symmetry.

Different shapes have different numbers of symmetry lines: a rectangle has 2, an equilateral triangle has 3, a regular hexagon has 6, and a circle has infinitely many — any line through its centre works!

Your turn. How many lines of symmetry does an equilateral triangle have?
Show answer
3 — one from each corner to the middle of the opposite side.

Rotational symmetry — spin without noticing

A shape has rotational symmetry if you can spin it less than a full turn and it looks exactly the same. The number of times it matches in one full turn is its order of rotational symmetry.

A square has order 4 (it matches every 90°). A regular pentagon has order 5. The letter S has order 2 (it looks the same upside down). Every shape has at least order 1 (a full turn always brings it back), but we only call it 'rotational symmetry' when the order is 2 or more.

🥝
Line symmetry and rotational symmetry are different talents! A shape can have one, both, or neither. A square has the full set — 4 lines of symmetry and order-4 rotation. The letter S is a neat opposite: order-2 rotation but no line of symmetry at all. Once you can name both kinds separately, you can size up any shape.
Your turn. What is the order of rotational symmetry of a square?
Show answer
4 — it matches itself every 90° (90°, 180°, 270°, 360°).

Mirror images — left becomes right

Hold something up to a mirror and it flips left-to-right — that flipped version is its mirror image. The letter b reflects into d; the word WOW still reads WOW (it's symmetric!), but 3 reflects into a backwards Ɛ. Mirror reflection is exactly the 'flip' you used to test line symmetry — a shape with line symmetry is its own mirror image across that line.

🥝
One honest catch about letters: whether a letter has a line of symmetry depends on the font! Everything we say here is for plain block capitals (the upright, even kind, like A B C). A swirly or slanted font can break the symmetry, so the letter answers below are in this block-letter font. Shapes are different — a perfect circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry, no font needed.
Your turn. In a mirror, the digit 2 appears reversed. In plain block capitals, which of these look the SAME in a vertical mirror? (Try A, B, M, P.)
Show answer
In this block-letter font: A and M look the same (they have a vertical line of symmetry); B and P do not.

Geometry · Map Makers Core
BIG QUESTIONA pirate's note says: "From the old oak, walk to the spot 3 steps East and 2 steps North. Dig." Just two numbers — and X marks the exact spot. How can only two numbers pin down any place on a whole island, a city, or even the Earth? That's the power of coordinates, and you're about to become a master map-maker.

Hi explorer, it's Kiwi! 🗺️ This is the most adventurous geometry chapter. We'll learn to point in any direction, read a map grid, and then meet the brilliant coordinate plane — the idea that links shapes to numbers and runs every video game map, GPS, and graph in the world. Two numbers, infinite places. Let's explore!

Directions: the compass and turns

A compass shows the four main directions: North, East, South, West. Going clockwise from North: N → E → S → W. Between them sit NE, SE, SW, NW.

A handy memory phrase for the order clockwise from the top is Never Eat Soggy Weetabix (N, E, S, W). Turns are measured like angles: a quarter-turn is 90°, a half-turn is 180°, a full turn is 360°.

✏️ Worked example — which way after turning?
  1. You are facing North. You turn 90° clockwise (to the right).
  2. Clockwise from N is E, so you now face East.
  3. Turn another 90° clockwise → South. So 180° from North is South — a half-turn faces you the opposite way!
🥝
Clockwise means turning the way clock hands move (N→E→S→W). Anticlockwise is the reverse (N→W→S→E). Every 90° turn hops you one direction along; 180° is always straight behind you; 360° brings you right back where you started.
Your turn. You face West and make a 90° turn clockwise. Which direction do you face now?
Show answer
Clockwise from West is North, so you now face North.
Your turn. You face South. You turn 180°. Which way now?
Show answer
A half-turn flips you to the opposite direction → North.

✏️ Worked example — read map references

  2. The school 🏫 sits in column B, row 2 → its reference is B2.

The big idea: the coordinate plane

Map letters and numbers are good, but mathematicians use numbers for both directions — and that unlocks everything. We draw two number lines that cross at right angles:

• The x-axis runs across (left-right), like East on a compass.
• The y-axis runs up-down, like North.
• They cross at the origin, the point (0, 0), marked O.

Any point is named by an ordered pair (x, y): the x-number tells how far to go across first, then the y-number tells how far up. The order matters — that's why it's called an ordered pair!

🥝
Golden rule, never forget it: x before y, across before up — go along the corridor before you climb the stairs. The point (3, 2) means 'across 3, up 2', which is a totally different spot from (2, 3) ('across 2, up 3'). Order is everything.

✏️ Worked example — plot the point (4, 3)
  1. Start at the origin O (0, 0).
  2. The first number is x = 4: move 4 units across (to the right).
  3. The second number is y = 3: from there move 3 units up.
  4. Mark the point — that's P(4, 3). Across first, up second, every time.

Show answer

Reading coordinates — what point is that?

Going the other way is just as easy. To read a point's coordinates: see how far across it is (that's x), then how far up (that's y), and write (x, y).

✏️ Worked example — read the coordinates of point A
  1. Look at point A. How far across is it? 2 → x = 2.
  2. How far up is it? 3 → y = 3.
  3. So A = (2, 3). Always write across-number first.

Show answer

Paths & distance on a grid

Coordinates let us measure journeys. To find the distance along a straight horizontal line, subtract the x-values; along a straight vertical line, subtract the y-values.

✏️ Worked example — how far is the path A → B → C?
  1. A(1, 1) to B(1, 4): the x stays 1, so it's vertical. Distance = 4 − 1 = 3 units up.
  2. B(1, 4) to C(5, 4): the y stays 4, so it's horizontal. Distance = 5 − 1 = 4 units across.
  3. Total path length = 3 + 4 = 7 units. (Like walking 3 blocks up then 4 blocks over.)
🥝
A surprise peek ahead: A and C are 3 up and 4 across apart. The straight-line shortcut from A to C (the diagonal) turns out to be exactly 5 units — because 3, 4, 5 make a special right-triangle trio you'll meet again. The path is 7, but the crow-flies distance is 5. Geometry is full of these hidden gems! ✨
Your turn. What is the distance from (2, 1) to (2, 7) along the grid?
Show answer
Same x (vertical line): 7 − 1 = 6 units.

BIG QUESTIONA cafe offers 5 milkshake flavours and 4 toppings. You happily think “I'll try a different shake-and-topping every day.” How many days can you go before you must repeat? Welcome back, explorer! It's Kiwi. 🧮 In Level 2 you learned to count outfits by multiplying choices, and to list pairs without missing any. The golden idea stays simple — be organised, and let multiplication do the heavy lifting.

Quick recap: choices that follow each other get multiplied

Remember the rule from Level 2 — the Counting Principle: if one choice can be made in a ways and a second in b ways, then making both can happen in a × b ways. Let's see it with our cafe: 5 flavours, then for each flavour 4 toppings.

A small choice tree makes it visible — but notice we already knew the answer was 20 from 5 × 4, with no drawing needed:

Your turn. A pizza has 3 sizes and 6 toppings (one of each). How many different one-topping pizzas are there?
Show answer
Multiply the two stages: 3 × 6 = 18 pizzas.

The big leap: more than two stages

Here is what's new in Level 3. The principle doesn't stop at two choices — it keeps going for every stage. Just keep multiplying. Imagine getting dressed: 3 shirts, 2 trousers, 4 caps.

✏️ Worked example — a 4-digit PIN lock
  1. A lock has 4 wheels, each able to show any digit 0-9. Each wheel is a stage.
  2. Stage 1: 10 choices. Stage 2: 10. Stage 3: 10. Stage 4: 10.
  3. Multiply: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000 possible PINs (0000 up to 9999).
  4. Surprise! That's why a 4-digit PIN feels safe — a thief would have to try ten thousand codes. Add one more wheel and it jumps to 1,00,000. Each extra stage multiplies the danger away. 🔒
🥝
Big idea: more stages -> multiply more numbers. Counting grows explosively fast — that's the secret power (and surprise) of the Counting Principle. A handful of choices can make millions of possibilities.
Your turn. A meal deal is 1 starter (2 options) + 1 main (3 options) + 1 drink (4 options) + 1 dessert (2 options). How many different meal deals?
Show answer
Multiply every stage: 2 × 3 × 4 × 2 = 48 meal deals.

Forming numbers from digit cards

A classic Olympiad question: you hold the digit cards 3, 5, 7, 8. How many 3-digit numbers can you arrange if no card repeats (you only have one of each)? Think of three empty slots — hundreds, tens, ones — and fill them one at a time.

✏️ Worked example — counting the 3-digit numbers (no repeats)
  1. Hundreds slot: any of the 4 cards → 4 choices.
  2. Tens slot: one card is now used, so 3 cards remain.
  3. Ones slot: two cards used, so 2 remain.
  4. Multiply: 4 × 3 × 2 = 24 different numbers. (We'll trust the principle rather than write all 24!)

Now a famous twist that trips people up: what if a 0 is among the cards? A number cannot start with 0 (0 5 7 would just be 57). Suppose the cards are 0, 4, 7 and we want 3-digit numbers, no repeats.

✏️ Worked example — when 0 is a card -- fill the fussy slot first
  1. Hundreds slot first (it's the picky one — no 0 allowed): only 4 or 7 → 2 choices.
  2. Tens slot: 0 is now allowed here, and one card is used, so of the remaining 2 cards (including 0) → 2 choices.
  3. Ones slot: just 1 card left.
  4. Multiply: 2 × 2 × 1 = 4 numbers. List to be sure: 407, 470, 704, 740. Exactly 4! ✓
🥝
Golden habit: when one slot has a special rule (like “no leading zero”), fill that fussy slot first. Decide the hard thing while you still can, then the easy slots fall into place.
Your turn. Using cards 2, 4, 6, 8 (no repeats), how many 2-digit numbers can you make?
Show answer
Tens slot: 4 cards; ones slot: 3 left → 4 × 3 = 12 numbers. (They happen to all be even, since every card is an even digit.)

The big distinction: does ORDER matter?

Here is the most important idea in counting, and it decides everything. Sometimes order matters (we call these arrangements), and sometimes it doesn't (we call these selections). Watch the difference with three friends — Asha, Bina, Cara.

Arrangement (order matters): giving out a gold and a silver medal to 2 of them. Asha-gold/Bina-silver is a different result from Bina-gold/Asha-silver.

Let's list those 6 ordered results (first = gold, second = silver):

Selection (order doesn't matter): instead just choosing 2 friends for a team (no medals). Now Asha-with-Bina is the same team as Bina-with-Asha. So we must not count both. The teams are only:

✏️ Worked example — why selections are HALF the arrangements (here)
  1. Ordered medal results: 6 (we counted them).
  2. But each team of 2 was counted twice in that list — once as A→B and once as B→A. A pair of people can be lined up in 2 orders.
  3. So the number of teams = 6 ÷ 2 = 3. We divide away the repeats.
  4. Rule of thumb: arrangements count orderings; selections = arrangements ÷ (the number of ways the chosen group can be reordered).

The one question to always ask: “If I swap the order, is it a new result?” Yes → it's an arrangement (order matters). No → it's a selection (order doesn't), so beware of double-counting.

✏️ Worked example — counting the handshakes two ways

  2. Divide-the-double way: each of the 5 people shakes 4 others = 5 × 4 = 20 “ends”, but every handshake has 2 ends, so 20 ÷ 2 = 10.

Combinatorics · Smart Counting Stretch
BIG QUESTIONToss a coin — will it be Heads? Roll a die — will you get a six? You can't be sure… yet you're not totally guessing either. How do mathematicians pin down exactly how likely something is — and then turn into detectives who crack secret-code sums and 4,000-year-old number squares?
🥝
Hi again — Kiwi here! 🎲 This chapter has two joys. First, probability: maths can measure luck itself, putting every “what are the chances?” on a clean scale from 0 to 1. Then pure puzzle fun: cryptarithms (where letters hide digits) and magic squares (where every line adds to the same total). No scary formulas — just careful counting and clever reasoning.

Part A · Measuring chance

Before numbers, words. Every event sits somewhere on a chance line from impossible (it can never happen) to certain (it must happen):

To get a number, we count two things and make a fraction. It works tidily when every result is equally likely (a fair coin, a fair die):

    probability = (number of ways we WANT) ÷ (number of ways ALTOGETHER)

✏️ Worked example — tossing a fair coin
  1. Results altogether: 2 (Heads or Tails).
  2. Results we want (Heads): 1.
  3. P(Heads) = 1 ÷ 2 = 1/2 — an even chance, right in the middle of the line. 🪙

(Throughout this chapter we use our fair-coin model: exactly two equally likely results, Heads and Tails, and we ignore the wafer-thin chance the coin lands on its edge.)

🥝
A probability is always between 0 and 1. It can never be more than 1 — you can't be more than certain! And it's never less than 0.

Rolling a die

A fair die has 6 equally likely faces:

✏️ Worked example — the chance of an EVEN roll
  1. Even faces: 2, 4, 6 → 3 faces we want.
  2. Faces altogether: 6.
  3. P(even) = 3 ÷ 6 = 1/2. Same as a coin!
✏️ Worked example — the chance of rolling MORE than 4
  1. Faces more than 4: just 5 and 6 → 2 faces.
  2. P(more than 4) = 2 ÷ 6 = 1/3.
Your turn. On a fair die, what is the probability of rolling a prime number (2, 3 or 5)?
Show answer
Primes 2, 3, 5 → 3 faces of 6, so P = 3/6 = 1/2.

Spinners

This spinner has 4 equal slices: two Red, one Blue, one Green. Bigger share of the spinner → bigger probability.

✏️ Worked example — reading the spinner
  1. P(Red) = 2 of 4 = 2/4 = 1/2 (Red is the most likely — it fills half).
  2. P(Blue) = 1 of 4 = 1/4; P(Green) = 1 of 4 = 1/4.
  3. Check: 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 1. Every possible result, added up, makes 1. ✓
Your turn. On the same spinner, what is the probability of landing on a colour that is NOT Red?
Show answer
Not Red means Blue or Green → 2 slices of 4 = 2/4 = 1/2.

Marbles, and the complement trick

A bag holds 3 red and 2 blue marbles. You grab one without looking.

✏️ Worked example — red, blue, and the check
  1. P(red) = 3 of 5 = 3/5.
  2. P(blue) = 2 of 5 = 2/5.
  3. P(red) + P(blue) = 3/5 + 2/5 = 5/5 = 1. The two cover everything that can happen. ✓
🥝
Here's a Level-3 power move — the complement. Because all the chances add to 1, the chance of something NOT happening is 1 minus the chance it does: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Sometimes the “not” is far easier to count!
✏️ Worked example — using the complement
  1. A spinner lands on Red with probability 1/4, and Red and Blue are the only colours.
  2. Instead of measuring Blue directly, use the complement: P(Blue) = 1 − P(Red).
  3. P(Blue) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. ✓
Your turn. A bag has 4 red, 1 green and 1 yellow marble. What is the probability of pulling a marble that is not red?
Show answer
Not red = green or yellow = 2 of 6 = 2/6 = 1/3. (Check with the complement: red is 4/6 = 2/3, and 1 − 2/3 = 1/3. ✓)

Part B · Cryptarithms: letters hiding digits

Now we become code-breakers. In a cryptarithm, each letter stands for a secret digit, and the same letter is always the same digit. Crack the code so the sum is true. Start tiny:

✏️ Worked example — finding A
  1. A added three times makes 9, so 3 × A = 9.
  2. A = 9 ÷ 3 = 3. Check: 3 + 3 + 3 = 9. ✓

A detective's shortcut you'll use again and again: a digit added to itself is always even (doubling lands on 0, 2, 4, 6, 8…). So in A + A, the answer's last digit can only be even. That single clue throws away half the guesses!

Your turn. Could A + A = 7 ever be true for a single digit A? Why or why not?
Show answer
No — A + A is always even, but 7 is odd. Impossible.

The big one: AB + B = BA

Here AB is a 2-digit number (A tens, B ones), and we add the single digit B. The answer is BA — the same two digits, swapped. Reason it out column by column:

✏️ Worked example — cracking AB + B = BA — a clean proof, no guessing
  1. Write what the digits mean. AB is the number 10A + B; add the single digit B; the answer BA is 10B + A. So the sum says 10A + B + B = 10B + A, i.e. 10A + 2B = 10B + A.
  2. Tidy it up. Move the letters together: 10A − A = 10B − 2B, which gives 9A = 8B. One neat equation now carries the whole puzzle.
  3. Read off the only answer. 9 and 8 share no common factor, so for 9A = 8B the left side must be a multiple of 8 and the right a multiple of 9. With single digits and A ≥ 1, that forces A = 8 and B = 9 (then 9×8 = 8×9 = 72 — it balances).
  4. Check the whole sum: 89 + 9 = 98 = BA. ✓ The proof guarantees this is the one and only solution — we never had to try cases.
🥝
That's the difference between a guesser and a mathematician: instead of testing digit after digit, we turned the picture into the equation 9A = 8B and the answer fell out. A quick computer sweep of all 100 digit pairs agrees — only A = 8, B = 9 works. The algebra already knew that.

One more pattern worth proving: AB + BA = (10A + B) + (10B + A) = 11A + 11B = 11 × (A + B) — always a multiple of 11. What kind of multiple depends on A + B:

• If A + B is less than 10, then 11 × (A + B) is a two-digit repeated number: 25 + 52 = 77, 16 + 61 = 77 (both have A + B = 7), and 72 + 27 = 99 (A + B = 9).
• If A + B is 10 or more, the total spills into three digits — still a multiple of 11: 58 + 85 = 143 = 11 × 13. The repeated-digit look only happens while A + B stays a single digit.

Your turn. In the code BB + B with B = 2 (BB means the two-digit number 22), what is the total?
Show answer
BB = 22, plus B = 2, so 22 + 2 = 24.

Part C · Magic squares

A magic square is a grid where every row, every column, and both diagonals add up to the same total — the magic number. Here is the oldest and most famous, the Lo Shu square (over 4,000 years old!), using 1 to 9 exactly once each:

✏️ Worked example — checking the magic number
  1. Top row: 2 + 7 + 6 = 15.
  2. Middle column: 7 + 5 + 3 = 15.
  3. Main diagonal: 2 + 5 + 8 = 15. Every line makes 15!
  4. Why 15? The digits 1 to 9 add to 45, shared over 3 rows: 45 ÷ 3 = 15. And the centre is always the magic number ÷ 3 = 5.

The real fun is completing a half-finished square — you don't guess, you work out each blank. Magic number = 15; find the three “?”:

✏️ Worked example — solving the blanks, one line at a time
  1. Left column (2, ?, 4) must total 15: ? = 15 − 6 = 9.
  2. Bottom row (4, ?, 8) must total 15: ? = 15 − 12 = 3.
  3. Check the middle row: 9 + 5 + 1 = 15. ✓ The finished square is the famous one.
🥝
The secret to every magic square: hunt for a row, column or diagonal with only one blank. With a single unknown you can always find it — magic number minus the known numbers. Fill it, and a fresh one-blank line usually opens up.
Your turn. A row of a magic square reads 6, ?, 8 and the magic number is 21. What is the missing number?
Show answer
6 + ? + 8 = 21, so ? = 21 − 14 = 7.

Combinatorics · Sock Drawer Logic Stretch
BIG QUESTIONYour sock drawer is a jumble of black and white socks, and the room is pitch dark. You can't see a thing. What is the smallest number of socks you must grab to be 100% sure you're holding a matching pair? The answer is wonderfully small — and it reveals one of the most powerful ideas in all of mathematics.
🥝
Hello, super-sleuth — Kiwi here! 🕵 This is the reasoning chapter, and it's pure thinking joy: no formulas to memorise, just careful logic. We'll meet a surprising guarantee called the Pigeonhole Principle, solve deduction grids, untangle who-ranks-where, read clocks and calendars, sort out families, and learn to work backwards. Go slowly, one clue at a time, and write down what you know.

Part A · The sock-drawer surprise (Pigeonhole)

Back to the dark room. There are only 2 colours of sock. Let's think about the worst possible luck. You pull one sock — say black. You pull another — the meanest luck gives you white. Now you hold one of each, still no pair. But the third sock must be black or white — either way it matches one you already hold!

✏️ Worked example — why 3 socks guarantee a pair
  1. There are only 2 colours (think of them as 2 “boxes”).
  2. Each sock you grab must go into one of those 2 boxes.
  3. Grab 3 socks for 2 boxes — by sheer force, some box gets 2 socks. Those 2 are the same colour: a pair!
  4. So 3 is the magic number. Not 2 (you might get one of each), but 3 makes a pair certain. ✓
🥝
That's the Pigeonhole Principle, and it's beautifully simple: if you put more items than boxes, some box must hold at least two items. More pigeons than holes → some hole is shared. It guarantees a result without you knowing anything about which sock is which — that's the surprise!

Change the numbers and the idea still works. With 3 colours of sock, the worst luck gives one of each (3 socks, no pair), so the 4th sock guarantees a pair. The rule: (number of colours) + 1 socks always guarantees a matching pair.

✏️ Worked example — a trickier pigeonhole: sharing a birth month
  1. There are 12 months — think of them as 12 boxes.
  2. Put 13 people into those 12 month-boxes (by their birth month).
  3. 13 people, 12 boxes → some month-box holds at least 2 people.
  4. So in any group of 13 people, at least two share a birth month — guaranteed, every time. 🎉
Your turn. A drawer has socks of 4 colours, all mixed up in the dark. How many socks must you grab to be sure of a matching pair?
Show answer
Worst luck = one of each colour (4 socks, no pair), so the next one matches: 5 socks. (Rule: colours + 1.)

Part B · Logic-grid deduction

A logic puzzle gives you clues and asks you to match things up — like “who owns which pet?” A grid keeps your thinking tidy: mark ◯ for a definite YES and × for a definite NO. Each clue lets you fill in some marks, and YES in one cell forces NO across the rest of that row and column.

Three friends — Amy, Ben, Cara — each own one pet: a cat, a dog, or a fish. Clues: (1) Ben owns the dog. (2) Amy does not own the cat.

✏️ Worked example — finishing the deduction
  1. Clue 1: Ben owns the Dog → ◯ at Ben/Dog. So Ben is not Cat or Fish, and nobody else owns the Dog (× down the Dog column).
  2. Clue 2: Amy is not the Cat (× at Amy/Cat). The only pets left for Amy are Dog or Fish — but Dog is taken, so Amy owns the Fish.
  3. What's left: the Cat must belong to Cara (the last person, the last pet).
  4. Answer: Ben–Dog, Amy–Fish, Cara–Cat. Every clue fits, and it's the only solution. ✓
🥝
The grid's secret: once you write ◯ (YES) in a cell, immediately put × across the rest of that row and that column — one person, one pet. Then re-read the clues; new YESes appear, and the puzzle unravels itself.
Your turn. Three children — Raj, Sam, Tia — like one fruit each: apple, banana, cherry. Sam likes the banana, and Raj does not like the apple. Who likes what?
Show answer
Sam = banana. Raj is not apple and banana is taken, so Raj = cherry. That leaves Tia = apple.

Part C · Ranking & position

A queue puzzle: Sam is 9th from the front and 38th from the back of a line. How many people are in the line? The trap is to add 9 + 38 = 47 — but that counts Sam twice (once from each end).

✏️ Worked example — the ranking rule
  1. Sam is counted in the “9 from the front” and in the “38 from the back” — that's Sam double-counted.
  2. Total = (position from front) + (position from back) − 1.
  3. Total = 9 + 38 − 1 = 46 people. The “−1” removes the double count. ✓
🥝
Position rule: front-rank + back-rank − 1 = total. The −1 is because the person themselves is counted from both directions. The same idea fixes “how many between?” puzzles — always check who's being counted twice.
Your turn. In a row, Meena is 7th from the left and 12th from the right. How many children are in the row?
Show answer
7 + 12 − 1 = 18 children.

Part D · Clocks, angles & calendars

A clock face is a circle, so all the way round is 360°. There are 12 hour marks, so the gap between two neighbouring numbers is 360 ÷ 12 = 30°. That single fact lets us measure the angle between the hands.

✏️ Worked example — the angle between the hands at 3:00
  1. The hour hand is on 3, the minute hand on 12 — that's 3 gaps apart.
  2. Each gap is 30°, so the angle = 3 × 30 = 90° (a perfect right angle!).
🥝
Quick angle facts: 12:00 → the hands overlap, 0°. 3:00 and 9:00 → 90° (right angle). 6:00 → 180° (straight line). Between numbers the hour hand creeps along too — it moves a little every minute — which is why 3:30 isn't exactly 90°.
Your turn. What is the angle between the clock hands at 9:00?
Show answer
9 and 12 are 3 gaps apart, so 3 × 30 = 90° (a right angle).

Day-of-the-week jumps

The 7 days repeat forever, so jumping exactly 7 days (or 14, or 21…) lands you on the same day. To find a future day, divide the jump by 7 and only the remainder matters.

✏️ Worked example — what day is 10 days after Monday?
  1. 10 = 7 + 3. The 7 brings us right back to Monday.
  2. Then count on the remainder, 3: Monday → Tue (1) → Wed (2) → Thu (3).
  3. So 10 days after Monday is a Thursday. ✓
🥝
Calendar trick: only the remainder after dividing by 7 changes the day. 15 days ahead? 15 = 7 + 7 + 1, remainder 1, so just 1 day later than today. Months vary in length (30 or 31 days; February has 28, or 29 in a leap year).
Your turn. Today is Friday. Your birthday is in exactly 15 days. On which day of the week is it?
Show answer
15 = 7 + 7 + 1, remainder 1, so 1 day after Friday → Saturday.

Part E · Family puzzles (who is who?)

A relationship puzzle describes people and asks how two are related. The best tool is a little family tree — a line going down means “parent of”, and a line across between two boxes means “married to”.

🥝
Handy word-list: parent's brother = uncle, parent's sister = aunt, aunt/uncle's child = cousin, your brother's or sister's child = your nephew (boy) / niece (girl), and your parent's parent = grandparent.
✏️ Worked example — “The man's mother is the only daughter of my mother.” (a woman is speaking)
  1. Find the only daughter of my mother. The speaker is the woman, so her mother's only daughter is the woman herself.
  2. The sentence says the man's mother is that person — so the man's mother is the woman.
  3. If the woman is the man's mother, she is his mother. ✓ (Tip: phrases like “the only daughter of my mother” often point straight back at the speaker.)
Your turn. Pointing to a boy, Mr Suresh says, “He is the son of the only son of my mother.” How is Suresh related to the boy?
Show answer
The only son of Suresh's mother is Suresh himself, so the boy is Suresh's son — Suresh is the boy's father.

Part F · Working backwards

Some puzzles tell you the end and ask for the start. The trick is to run the steps in reverse, turning each operation into its opposite: undo × with ÷, and undo + with − — in the backwards order.

✏️ Worked example — number machine: double, then add 6, gives 20
  1. Run it backwards, last step first. The last thing done was +6; undo it: 20 − 6 = 14.
  2. The step before was ×2; undo it: 14 ÷ 2 = 7.
  3. So the starting number was 7. Check forwards: 7 × 2 = 14, + 6 = 20. ✓
🥝
Working backwards is a real problem-solving superpower — the same one champions use. Whenever you know the finish and want the start, reverse the journey and flip every operation to its opposite.
Your turn. I think of a number, add 5, then multiply by 3, and get 36. What was my number?
Show answer
Backwards: undo ×3 first → 36 ÷ 3 = 12; undo +5 → 12 − 5 = 7. (Check: 7 + 5 = 12, × 3 = 36. ✓)

Data Handling · Data Detectives Core
BIG QUESTIONFive friends compare pocket money: ₹10, ₹30, ₹20, ₹40, ₹0. “That's not fair!” cries the one with nothing. If they pooled it all and split it equally, how much would each get? That single “fair share” number is one of the most useful ideas in maths — and by the end of this chapter you'll read it straight out of charts and graphs.
🥝
Hello, data detective — Kiwi here! 📊 Numbers are clues, and the right picture makes the clues leap out. In this final chapter you'll learn the three kinds of average (mean, median, mode), then read and draw bar charts, pictographs and pie charts — finding the most, the least, the total, and the story the data is trying to tell.

Part A · The mean: a fair share

The mean (the everyday “average”) is what each one would get if you shared everything equally. Imagine three towers of blocks — 3, 5 and 1 — and you slide blocks across until all towers are the same height:

✏️ Worked example — the recipe for the mean
  1. Add all the values: 3 + 5 + 1 = 9 blocks altogether.
  2. Divide by how many there are: 9 ÷ 3 = 3.
  3. So the mean is 3 — the fair, level share. Rule: mean = total ÷ (how many).

Back to the pocket money: ₹10 + ₹30 + ₹20 + ₹40 + ₹0 = ₹100 shared among 5 friends = ₹20 each. Notice the mean (₹20) isn't anyone's actual amount — it's the balancing point of them all.

🥝
Two things the mean loves to do: (1) it lands between the smallest and biggest value, never outside; (2) it's the size every value would be if they were all equal. A great check: if your mean is bigger than every number, you've slipped somewhere!
✏️ Worked example — a useful twist: finding a missing value
  1. Over 5 days a runner's steps (in thousands) were 12, 15, 9, 8, and one more day that's smudged. The mean was 12.
  2. If the mean of 5 numbers is 12, their total must be 12 × 5 = 60.
  3. The four known days add to 12 + 15 + 9 + 8 = 44.
  4. So the smudged day = 60 − 44 = 16 (thousand steps). Working from the mean back to a total is a real detective move! ✓
Your turn. Find the mean of 6, 10, 8.
Show answer
Total = 6 + 10 + 8 = 24; there are 3 numbers; mean = 24 ÷ 3 = 8.

Part B · Median & mode (two more averages)

The median is the middle value when the numbers are lined up in order. Line up 3, 7, 2, 9, 5 from smallest to biggest and look at the one in the centre:

✏️ Worked example — finding the median (an odd-sized list)
  1. Sort the numbers: 2, 3, 5, 7, 9.
  2. There are 5 numbers, so the middle one is the 3rd: 5.
  3. The median is 5 — half the numbers are below it, half above.

If there's an even number of values there are two in the middle — the median is their mean (halfway between). For 2, 4, 6, 8 the two middles are 4 and 6, so the median is (4 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5.

The mode is the value that appears most often — the most “popular” number. In 3, 5, 5, 2, 5, 1 the number 5 shows up three times, more than any other, so the mode is 5.

🥝
Three averages, three jobs: the mean is the fair share (add then divide); the median is the middle in order; the mode is the most common. A shoe shop cares about the mode (which size sells most!), while a teacher sharing sweets cares about the mean.
Your turn. Find the mode and the median of the shoe sizes 6, 7, 7, 8, 7, 9.
Show answer
Mode = 7 (it appears three times). Sorted: 6, 7, 7, 7, 8, 9 — six numbers, two middles (7 and 7), so median = (7 + 7) ÷ 2 = 7.

Kangaroo Thinking Tricks

Kangaroo · Brain Benders Olympiad
BIG QUESTIONHere is a puzzle that looks impossible. Five glasses stand on a table, all the right way up. Each move, you must turn over exactly two of them. Can you ever get all five upside-down? Try it… you'll fail every time. But a champion doesn't just try harder — they prove it's impossible in one line. By the end of this chapter you'll see exactly why — and you'll think like a Kangaroo. 🦘
🥝
Hello again, explorer — it's Kiwi! You've climbed a long way since the Level 2 Kangaroo Corner. That was pitched at the younger Écolier stage (Grades 3–4). Now we step up to Benjamin-style thinking — the kind of reasoning the Math Kangaroo contest sets for Grades 5–6, taken by students across many countries. We'll keep that friendly format: every problem is multiple choice with five answers, (A) to (E), grouped into 3-point warm-ups, 4-point thinkers and 5-point stars, and no calculators. But the thinking goes deeper. These nine strategies are the real tools that crack these puzzles. Let's collect them. 🚀

Trick 1 🦜 — Parity: the odd/even superpower

Parity just means whether a number is odd or even. The Kangaroo secret is this: in many puzzles, some quantity can only change in a way that keeps its parity the same. If the start and the goal have different parities, the goal is impossible — no matter how hard you try!

Back to our five glasses. Count how many are upside-down. At the start, that count is 0 (even). Each move flips exactly two glasses, so the down-count changes by −2, 0, or +2 — it always stays even. But “all five down” needs the down-count to be 5, which is odd. Even can never become odd by adding 2s. Impossible!

✏️ Worked example — why 5 glasses can never all go down (flipping 2 at a time)
  1. Track one thing: how many glasses are upside-down. Start = 0, which is even.
  2. Every legal move turns over exactly 2 glasses, changing the down-count by an even amount.
  3. An even number plus or minus even numbers is always even — the parity is locked.
  4. The target “all 5 down” needs down-count = 5 (odd). Locked-even can never reach odd. So it is impossible. ✓

With an even number of glasses (say 6), the goal is reachable — because now 6 is even, matching the locked parity.

🥝
Parity also cracks the classic “put + and − signs in front of 1, 2, 3, …, 10 to make 0” puzzle. The total 1+2+…+10 = 55 is odd. Flipping any ‘+’ to a ‘−’ changes the sum by an even amount, so every result stays odd — and 0 is even. Impossible! (With 1…7 the total is 28, even, so there it can be done.)
Your turn. Seven lamps are all OFF. Each move you must switch exactly two of them. Can you ever get all seven ON? Explain in one line.
Show answer
No. The number of ON lamps starts at 0 (even) and each move changes it by an even amount, so it stays even — but “all 7 on” is odd. Parity forbids it.

Trick 2 🦜 — Invariants: find what never changes

Parity is really one example of a bigger idea: an invariant — some quantity that stays the same (or changes in a totally predictable way) no matter what moves you make. Spot the invariant and a wild-looking puzzle becomes easy.

Here's a number game. The numbers 1, 2, 3, …, 10 are written on a board. Each move, you rub out two of them, say a and b, and write the single number a + b − 1 instead. You repeat until just one number is left. What is it?

✏️ Worked example — the disappearing-numbers game
  1. Don't simulate — hunt for what's invariant. Watch the sum of all numbers on the board.
  2. When you replace a, b with a+b−1, the sum changes from (…+a+b) to (…+a+b−1) — it goes down by exactly 1 every move.
  3. Start sum = 1+2+…+10 = 55. You make moves until one number remains: that's 10 − 1 = 9 moves, each dropping the sum by 1.
  4. Final number = 55 − 9 = 46. The order you pick the numbers never matters. ✓
🥝
The magic of an invariant: you get the answer without doing the steps. You found a quantity (the sum) that obeys a simple rule (drops by 1 each move), so you could leap straight to the end. Champions always ask: “What stays the same here?”
Your turn. Same game, but on the board are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and each move you write a + b − 1. What single number is left at the end?
Show answer
Sum starts at 21; there are 6 − 1 = 5 moves, each dropping the sum by 1. Final = 21 − 5 = 16.

Trick 3 🦜 — The Pigeonhole Principle

Possibly the most beautiful idea in this whole chapter, and it sounds almost silly: if you put more pigeons than holes, some hole must hold at least two pigeons. Put 5 pigeons into 4 holes — one hole is guaranteed to get 2 or more. You can't avoid it!

The power is choosing the right “pigeons” and “holes”. Socks in a drawer are a favourite. If a drawer has socks of 3 colours, how many must you grab in the dark to be certain of a matching pair?

✏️ Worked example — guaranteeing a matching pair of socks (3 colours)
  1. Let the holes be the 3 colours. Each sock you pull is a pigeon dropped into its colour-hole.
  2. With 3 socks you might be unlucky: one of each colour, no pair yet.
  3. Pull one more — the 4th sock must match a colour you already have (only 3 colours exist). Pigeonhole!
  4. So 4 socks guarantee a pair. The rule: (number of holes) + 1. ✓
🥝
💡 Always ask worst case: imagine an enemy handing you the unluckiest socks possible. Pigeonhole says even the enemy must eventually give you a repeat. A sharper version: if you put more than 2×(holes) pigeons in, some hole gets at least 3, and so on.

A number version: choose any 5 numbers from 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. Must two of them add up to 9? Pair the numbers by what makes 9: {1,8}, {2,7}, {3,6}, {4,5} — that's 4 pairs (the holes). Picking 5 numbers means two land in the same pair — and those two sum to 9. Yes, guaranteed.

Your turn. A box has red, green, blue and yellow balls all mixed up. Reaching in without looking, how many balls must you take to be sure of two the same colour?
Show answer
4 colours = 4 holes, so 4 + 1 = 5 balls guarantee a matching pair.

Trick 4 🦜 — Working backwards (undo the whole story)

Some problems tell a story forwards and ask for the start. The trick is to begin at the end and undo each step in reverse: + becomes −, × becomes ÷, ‘half of’ becomes ‘double’. Benjamin loves multi-step ones.

✏️ Worked example — a number is ×3, then +7, then ÷2, then −4, and the result is 10
  1. Start at the end, 10, and reverse the last step first. Last was −4, so undo with +4: 10 + 4 = 14.
  2. Before that was ÷2; undo with ×2: 14 × 2 = 28.
  3. Before that was +7; undo with −7: 28 − 7 = 21.
  4. Before that was ×3; undo with ÷3: 21 ÷ 3 = 7.
  5. The secret start was 7. Check forwards: 7×3=21, +7=28, ÷2=14, −4=10. ✓
🥝
Working backwards shines in “giving away half” puzzles too. I had some marbles. I gave away half plus one to Ami, then half of what was left plus one to Bo, and ended with 3. Reverse it: before Bo, (3+1)×2 = 8; before Ami, (8+1)×2 = 18. I started with 18 marbles!
Your turn. Mira thinks of a number, doubles it, then adds 3, and the result is 16. Wait — can her number be a whole number? If yes, what is it?
Show answer
Undo: 16 − 3 = 13, then 13 ÷ 2 = 6.5. That's not a whole number, so if she meant a whole number she made a slip. (If instead the result were 17: 17−3=14, 14÷2 = 7.) Working backwards even catches impossible stories!

Trick 5 🦜 — Clever counting: count the opposite

When something is hard to count directly, count the opposite (the “complement”) and subtract from the total. It's often far easier — this is one of the most useful shortcuts in all of Kangaroo.

How many whole numbers from 1 to 100 are not divisible by 3 or 5? Counting them one by one is painful. Instead, count the ones that are divisible by 3 or 5, then take that away from 100.

✏️ Worked example — how many of 1–100 are NOT divisible by 3 or 5
  1. Multiples of 3 up to 100: 100 ÷ 3 = 33 (ignore the remainder) → 33.
  2. Multiples of 5: 100 ÷ 5 = 20.
  3. But multiples of 15 got counted twice (they're in both), so subtract them once: 100 ÷ 15 = 6.
  4. Divisible by 3 or 5 = 33 + 20 − 6 = 47 (this is the ‘add the two, take back the overlap’ rule).
  5. So not divisible by 3 or 5 = 100 − 47 = 53. ✓
🥝
That “add the two, then subtract the overlap” move has a fancy name — inclusion–exclusion — but you just used it like a pro. Another complement gem: to count numbers with a certain digit, it's usually easier to count the ones without it and subtract.

Geometry has a complement trick too. The number of diagonals of a shape with n corners is n(n−3) ÷ 2 — because each corner joins to all others except itself and its 2 neighbours. An octagon (8 corners) has 8×5÷2 = 20 diagonals.

Your turn. How many whole numbers from 1 to 20 are not multiples of 4?
Show answer
Multiples of 4 up to 20: 20 ÷ 4 = 5 of them. So not multiples of 4 = 20 − 5 = 15.

Trick 6 🦜 — Seeing in 3D: the painted cube

Kangaroo loves cubes you must picture in your head. The champion move is to sort the little cubes by where they live: corners (3 faces showing), edges (2 faces), face-centres (1 face), and buried inside (0 faces). For an n×n×n cube this never changes its pattern.

✏️ Worked example — a 4×4×4 cube is painted all over — how many little cubes have 0, 1, 2, 3 painted faces?
  1. Corners always show 3 faces. A cube has 8 corners → 8 cubes with 3 painted faces (true for any n).
  2. Edges show 2 faces. Each of the 12 edges has (n−2) middle cubes → 12×(4−2) = 24 cubes with 2 faces.
  3. Face-centres show 1 face. Each of the 6 faces has (n−2)² centre cubes → 6×2² = 24 cubes with 1 face.
  4. Buried cubes show 0 faces — they form a smaller (n−2)³ cube inside: 2³ = 8 with no paint.
  5. Check the total: 8 + 24 + 24 + 8 = 64 = 4³. ✓ Every cube accounted for!
🥝
Memorise the four formulas — they unlock every painted-cube question:
• 3 faces: always 8 (the corners).
• 2 faces: 12×(n−2) (the edges).
• 1 face: 6×(n−2)² (the face-centres).
• 0 faces: (n−2)³ (buried inside).
Your turn. A 5×5×5 cube is painted all over and cut into 125 little cubes. How many have exactly one painted face? And how many have none?
Show answer
One face = 6×(5−2)² = 6×9 = 54. None = (5−2)³ = 3³ = 27.

Trick 7 🦜 — Counting routes on a grid

A town is a grid; you start bottom-left and walk to top-right, only ever going right or up. How many shortest routes are there? In Level 2 you added corner-by-corner. Benjamin gives you a faster engine — and asks you to handle closed roads too.

✏️ Worked example — how many shortest routes cross a 3×3 grid of blocks?
  1. Label every corner with the number of ways to reach it. Edges are all 1 (one straight way).
  2. Each inside corner = (ways from below) + (ways from the left), exactly like building Pascal's triangle.
  3. Add your way up; the top-right corner reads 20.
  4. The fast formula: a route is just 3 R's and 3 U's in some order — choose which 3 of the 6 steps are R's. That count is “6 choose 3” = 20. Same answer, instantly. ✓
🥝
The engine: on a grid that is m wide and n tall, the number of shortest right/up routes is “(m+n) choose m” — the number of ways to arrange m rights among m+n steps. (m+n choose m means (m+n)! ÷ (m! × n!).) For a 4×4 grid that's “8 choose 4” = 70 routes.

Now the Benjamin twist: a road is closed at one corner. Count routes through the bad corner and subtract them (complement again!). Routes avoiding it = (all routes) − (routes forced through it).

✏️ Worked example — routes across the 3×3 grid that avoid the closed corner
  1. All routes with no closure = 20 (from above).
  2. Routes forced through the closed corner = (ways to reach it) × (ways from it to the End). To that corner: “2 choose 1” = 2. From it onward: “4 choose 2” = 6.
  3. So routes through the bad corner = 2 × 6 = 12.
  4. Routes that avoid it = 20 − 12 = 8. ✓
Your turn. On a grid 4 wide and 2 tall, how many shortest right/up routes are there (no closures)?
Show answer
“(4+2) choose 4” = “6 choose 4” = 15 routes.

Trick 8 🦜 — Logical elimination with clues

Detective problems give you clues; your job is to cross out the impossible until one answer survives. Two master techniques: line things up in order, and test the cases one by one.

Ordering. Five children take a test, all with different scores. Mira scored higher than Ravi, Ravi higher than Tia, Sam higher than Mira, and Ela scored the lowest. Who came third?

✏️ Worked example — ordering the five scores
  1. Chain the ‘higher than’ clues: Sam > Mira > Ravi > Tia.
  2. Ela is the lowest, so she sits at the very bottom, below Tia.
  3. The full order, highest to lowest: Sam, Mira, Ravi, Tia, Ela.
  4. Third place is Ravi. ✓

Testing cases. A cookie vanished. Four friends each speak, but exactly one of them is telling the truth. Tom: “Sam took it.” Sam: “I did not.” Pia: “I did not.” Ron: “Tom took it.” Who took the cookie?

✏️ Worked example — the cookie thief (exactly one statement is true)
  1. Try each suspect as the thief and count how many statements come out true; we need exactly one true.
  2. If Tom took it: Tom F, Sam T, Pia T, Ron T → 3 true. ✗
  3. If Sam took it: Tom T, Sam F, Pia T, Ron F → 2 true. ✗
  4. If Pia took it: Tom F, Sam T, Pia F, Ron F → 1 true. ✓
  5. If Ron took it: Tom F, Sam T, Pia T, Ron F → 2 true. ✗
  6. Only the case “Pia took it” gives exactly one true statement. The thief is Pia!
🥝
Two golden detective habits: (1) turn comparison clues into one long chain — the answer reads straight off an end; (2) when statements are tangled, just test every possibility and keep the one that fits all the clues. Slow and sure beats clever guessing.
Your turn. Ana, Ben and Com each play one sport: chess, tennis or swimming. Ana does not play chess. Ben plays tennis. Com does not swim. Who plays what?
Show answer
Ben = tennis. Com isn't swimming and tennis is taken, so Com = chess. That leaves Ana = swimming. (Ana=swim, Ben=tennis, Com=chess.)

Trick 9 🦜 — Digit sense: divisibility & last digits

Number-sense tricks turn scary-looking numbers into easy ones. Two you must own:

Divisibility by 9 (and 3): a number is divisible by 9 exactly when its digit sum is divisible by 9 (for 3, when the digit sum is divisible by 3). So you can fill in a missing digit without long division.

✏️ Worked example — the 3-digit number 4 ⬚ 2 is divisible by 9 — what is the missing digit?
  1. Divisible by 9 means the digit sum is a multiple of 9.
  2. Digit sum = 4 + ? + 2 = 6 + ?.
  3. The next multiple of 9 at or above 6 is 9, so 6 + ? = 9 → ? = 3.
  4. Check: 432 ÷ 9 = 48 exactly. ✓

Last-digit cycles: the units digit of the powers of a number repeats in a short loop. For powers of 7 the last digits cycle 7, 9, 3, 1 and then repeat — a loop of length 4. To find the last digit of a big power, you only need its position in the loop.

✏️ Worked example — what is the last digit of 7¹⁰⁰?
  1. List the last-digit loop for 7: 7¹→7, 7²→9, 7³→3, 7⁴→1, then it repeats. Loop length = 4.
  2. Find where exponent 100 lands: 100 ÷ 4 = 25 remainder 0. A remainder of 0 means we are at the end of a loop — the 4th position.
  3. The 4th last-digit in the loop is 1.
  4. So 7¹⁰⁰ ends in 1. (No need to compute the giant number!) ✓
🥝
Handy loops to recognise: powers of 2 → 2, 4, 8, 6 (length 4); powers of 3 → 3, 9, 7, 1 (length 4); powers of 9 → 9, 1 (length 2); powers of 5 always end in 5; powers of 6 always end in 6. Match the exponent to its spot in the loop using the remainder.
Your turn. What is the last digit of 3⁹⁹ (3 to the power 99)?
Show answer
Loop for 3 is 3, 9, 7, 1 (length 4). 99 ÷ 4 = 24 remainder 3 → the 3rd spot → 7.

Now climb the Benjamin ladder 🦜

Time to use your nine tools. These rise from gentle to genuinely tricky — the same spirit as a Kangaroo-style round. Try each before you peek, and always ask: which trick fits here?

Remember
  1. In the painted-cube method, how many little cubes always have exactly 3 painted faces?
    answer Always 8 — the corners.
  2. Pigeonhole: 7 pigeons go into 6 holes. At least one hole holds how many?
    answer At least 2 (7 > 6).
  3. What is the last-digit loop for powers of 2?
    answer 2, 4, 8, 6, then it repeats (length 4).
  4. A number's digit sum is 18. Is it divisible by 9?
    answer Yes — 18 is a multiple of 9, so the number is too.
  5. To count shortest right/up routes on an m×n grid, which count do you use?
    answer “(m+n) choose m” — arrange m rights among m+n steps.
Understand
  1. Why can 5 glasses (all up) never all be turned down if you must flip exactly 2 each time?
    answer The number of down glasses starts even (0) and changes by an even amount each move, so it stays even; 5 is odd — impossible (parity).
  2. Socks come in 4 colours. How many must you grab in the dark to be sure of a matching pair?
    answer 4 + 1 = 5 socks.
  3. Numbers 1…6 are on a board; each move erase a, b and write a+b−1. Final number?
    answer Sum 21, five moves dropping 1 each → 21 − 5 = 16.
  4. How many whole numbers from 1 to 100 are NOT divisible by 3 or 5?
    answer Div by 3 or 5 = 33 + 20 − 6 = 47, so not = 100 − 47 = 53.
  5. Last digit of 2⁵⁰ (2 to the 50)?
    answer Loop 2,4,8,6; 50 ÷ 4 = 12 r 2 → 2nd spot → 4.
Apply
  1. A 4×4×4 cube is painted and split into 64 little cubes. How many have exactly 2 painted faces?
    answer Edges: 12 × (4 − 2) = 24 cubes.
  2. On a 3×3 grid of blocks, how many shortest right/up routes are there?
    answer “6 choose 3” = 20.
  3. A number is ×3, then +7, then ÷2, then −4, ending at 10. What was it?
    answer Undo backwards: 10+4=14, ×2=28, −7=21, ÷3=7.
  4. The 3-digit number 5 ◻ 4 is divisible by 9. Find the missing digit.
    answer 5 + ? + 4 = 9 + ? must be a multiple of 9 → ? = 0 (giving 504 = 9×56).
  5. Choose any 5 numbers from 1…8. Must two of them add to 9? Why?
    answer Yes. The pairs {1,8},{2,7},{3,6},{4,5} are 4 holes; 5 numbers force two into one pair, which sums to 9.
Analyze
  1. Can you put + and − signs in front of 1, 2, 3, …, 10 so the total is exactly 0? Explain.
    answer No. The plain sum 55 is odd; flipping a + to − changes the total by an even amount, so every result stays odd — but 0 is even. Parity forbids it.
  2. A 5×5×5 cube is painted all over. How many of the 125 little cubes have no paint?
    answer (5 − 2)³ = 3³ = 27.
  3. On a 3×3 grid of blocks, one corner is closed (the corner 1 right and 1 up from Start). How many shortest routes still reach the End?
    answer All 20, minus those through the bad corner: (2 choose 1)×(4 choose 2) = 2×6 = 12, so 20 − 12 = 8.
  4. Five kids scored differently. Sam > Mira > Ravi > Tia, and Ela is lowest. Who is 4th?
    answer Order: Sam, Mira, Ravi, Tia, Ela. Fourth is Tia.
  5. Last digit of 7⁷⁷ (7 to the 77)?
    answer Loop 7,9,3,1; 77 ÷ 4 = 19 r 1 → 1st spot → 7.
Create
  1. Invent your own “impossible by parity” puzzle with coins or switches, and say the one-line reason it can't be done.
    answer E.g. “9 cards face-down; each move flip exactly 2; can all face up?” No — face-up count stays even, but 9 is odd.
  2. Design a painted-cube question whose answer is 8 unpainted cubes. What size cube did you use?
    answer A 4×4×4 cube: (4−2)³ = 8 buried cubes have no paint.
  3. Make up a working-backwards puzzle whose secret start is 5. Give the forward steps and the final result.
    answer E.g. start 5: ×4 = 20, −3 = 17, so “a number is ×4 then −3 to give 17 — find it.” Undo: 17+3=20, ÷4 = 5. ✓

🦜 3-Point Warm-ups

Gentle starters to warm up your thinking. Each is worth 3 points.

3-Point Warm-ups
  1. Five glasses stand on a table, all the right way up. Each move you must turn over exactly two of them. Can you ever make all five upside-down?
    (A) Yes, in 3 moves   (B) Yes, in 5 moves   (C) No, it is impossible   (D) Yes, in 10 moves   (E) Only with luck
    answer (C) No, it is impossible. The number of down glasses starts at 0 (even) and each move changes it by an even amount, so it stays even — but “all 5 down” is odd. Parity forbids it.
  2. What is the last digit of 2¹⁰ (that is, 2 to the power 10)?
    (A) 2   (B) 4   (C) 6   (D) 8   (E) 0
    answer (B) 4. The last digits of powers of 2 loop 2, 4, 8, 6. Since 10 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 2, we want the 2nd in the loop → 4. (Indeed 2¹⁰ = 1024.)
  3. How many diagonals does a pentagon (a 5-sided shape) have?
    (A) 2   (B) 3   (C) 5   (D) 8   (E) 10
    answer (C) 5. Diagonals = n(n−3)÷2 = 5×2÷2 = 5. (Each corner joins the 2 non-neighbouring corners.)
  4. A town is a grid 2 blocks wide and 3 blocks tall. Going only right or up, how many shortest routes lead from the bottom-left to the top-right corner?
    (A) 5   (B) 6   (C) 8   (D) 10   (E) 12
    answer (D) 10. A route is 2 rights and 3 ups in some order: “5 choose 2” = 10.
  5. A 3 × 3 × 3 cube is painted all over, then split into 27 little cubes. How many little cubes have exactly two painted faces?
    (A) 6   (B) 8   (C) 9   (D) 12   (E) 18
    answer (D) 12. The two-face cubes sit on the edges: each of the 12 edges has 1 middle cube → 12.
  6. How many whole numbers from 1 to 20 are not multiples of 4?
    (A) 4   (B) 5   (C) 10   (D) 15   (E) 16
    answer (D) 15. Multiples of 4 up to 20 are 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 — that's 5. So 20 − 5 = 15 are not (counting by complement).
  7. A number is increased by 3, then the result is doubled, giving 16. What was the original number?
    (A) 3   (B) 4   (C) 5   (D) 6   (E) 8
    answer (C) 5. Work backwards: undo the doubling (16 ÷ 2 = 8), then undo the +3 (8 − 3 = 5). Check: 5 + 3 = 8, 8 × 2 = 16. ✓
  8. Socks of 4 colours are jumbled in a drawer. Reaching in in the dark, how many socks must you take to be certain of a matching pair?
    (A) 2   (B) 3   (C) 4   (D) 5   (E) 6
    answer (D) 5. Pigeonhole: 4 colours are 4 ‘holes’; 4 socks could be all different, but a 5th must repeat a colour — 5.

🦜 4-Point Thinkers

Now slow down and think. Each is worth 4 points — they reward a careful plan, not a quick guess.

4-Point Thinkers
  1. The numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 are written on a board. Each move, erase two of them, a and b, and write a + b − 1 in their place. After repeating until one number is left, what is that number?
    (A) 6   (B) 11   (C) 15   (D) 16   (E) 21
    answer (D) 16. The sum on the board drops by exactly 1 each move (invariant!). Start sum = 21; with 5 moves it falls to 21 − 5 = 16.
  2. What is the last digit of 3⁹⁹ (3 to the power 99)?
    (A) 1   (B) 3   (C) 7   (D) 9   (E) 27
    answer (C) 7. Powers of 3 end in 3, 9, 7, 1 (loop of 4). 99 ÷ 4 = 24 remainder 3 → the 3rd spot → 7.
  3. A 4 × 4 × 4 cube (64 little cubes) is painted all over, then split apart. How many little cubes have no painted face at all?
    (A) 0   (B) 4   (C) 8   (D) 16   (E) 27
    answer (C) 8. Peel off the painted shell; what remains is a (4−2)³ = 2×2×2 block = 8 unpainted cubes.
  4. How many whole numbers from 1 to 100 are divisible by 3 or 5?
    (A) 33   (B) 40   (C) 47   (D) 53   (E) 60
    answer (C) 47. Multiples of 3: 33. Of 5: 20. Of 15 (counted twice): 6. So 33 + 20 − 6 = 47.
  5. A town is a grid 4 blocks wide and 2 blocks tall. Moving only right or up, how many shortest routes go from corner to corner?
    (A) 6   (B) 10   (C) 12   (D) 15   (E) 20
    answer (D) 15. A route is 4 rights and 2 ups in some order: “6 choose 4” = “6 choose 2” = 15.
  6. In a class of 25 students, each has a birthday in one of the 12 months. The teacher says: ‘Some month is shared by at least ___ students.’ What is the largest number that must be true?
    (A) 1   (B) 2   (C) 3   (D) 4   (E) 12
    answer (C) 3. Pigeonhole: 25 students in 12 month-‘holes’. Even spread is 2 each (24), so the 25th forces some month to 3. (You can't guarantee 4.)
  7. The numbers 1, 2, 3, …, 10 are on a board. Each move, erase two, a and b, and write a + b − 1. What single number remains at the end?
    (A) 10   (B) 45   (C) 46   (D) 55   (E) 100
    answer (C) 46. The sum drops by 1 per move (invariant). Start 1+…+10 = 55; there are 9 moves → 55 − 9 = 46.
  8. How many two-digit numbers have digits that add up to exactly 9?
    (A) 7   (B) 8   (C) 9   (D) 10   (E) 11
    answer (C) 9. They are 18, 27, 36, 45, 54, 63, 72, 81, 90 — 9 numbers (the tens digit runs 1…9, and the units digit is whatever makes 9).

🦜 5-Point Stars

The big ones! Each 5-point star may need two tricks working together. Take your time, draw a picture, and check your answer makes sense.

5-Point Stars
  1. A 5 × 5 × 5 cube (125 little cubes) is painted on every outside face, then split apart. How many little cubes have exactly one painted face?
    (A) 27   (B) 36   (C) 48   (D) 54   (E) 96
    answer (D) 54. One-face cubes sit at face centres: 6 faces × (5−2)² = 6 × 9 = 54.
  2. What is the last digit of 7⁷⁷ (7 to the power 77)?
    (A) 1   (B) 3   (C) 7   (D) 9   (E) 49
    answer (C) 7. Powers of 7 end in 7, 9, 3, 1 (loop of 4). 77 ÷ 4 = 19 remainder 1 → the 1st spot → 7.
  3. A town is a 4 × 4 grid of blocks. Going only right or up, how many shortest routes go from Start to End if the centre corner is closed (see the ✖ in the picture)?
    (A) 8   (B) 16   (C) 34   (D) 36   (E) 70
    answer (C) 34. All routes = “8 choose 4” = 70. Routes through the closed centre = (4 choose 2)×(4 choose 2) = 6×6 = 36. So 70 − 36 = 34 avoid it.
  4. How many whole numbers from 1 to 200 are divisible by none of 2, 3 and 5?
    (A) 27   (B) 45   (C) 53   (D) 54   (E) 80
    answer (D) 54. By complement: divisible by 2, 3 or 5 = 100 + 66 + 40 − 33 − 20 − 13 + 6 = 146. So 200 − 146 = 54.
  5. A cookie vanished. Four friends speak, and exactly one of them is telling the truth. Tom: ‘Sam took it.’ Sam: ‘I did not.’ Pia: ‘I did not.’ Ron: ‘Tom took it.’ Who took the cookie?
    (A) Tom   (B) Sam   (C) Pia   (D) Ron   (E) Cannot tell
    answer (C) Pia. Test each thief and count true statements; only ‘Pia took it’ gives exactly one true (Sam's ‘I did not’). Tom→3 true, Sam→2, Ron→2.
  6. Five children scored differently on a test. Mira scored higher than Ravi, Ravi higher than Tia, and Sam higher than Mira; Ela scored the lowest. Who finished third?
    (A) Sam   (B) Mira   (C) Ravi   (D) Tia   (E) Ela
    answer (C) Ravi. Chain the clues: Sam > Mira > Ravi > Tia, with Ela last. The order is Sam, Mira, Ravi, Tia, Ela — third is Ravi.
  7. At a party, 10 people are seated in a circle and each person shakes hands with exactly 3 others. How many handshakes happen in total?
    (A) 10   (B) 13   (C) 15   (D) 20   (E) 30
    answer (C) 15. Count hand-ends: 10 people × 3 = 30, but each handshake is shared by 2 people, so 30 ÷ 2 = 15 handshakes.
  8. A 3-digit number is written with the same digit three times (like 222 or 888). For which digit A is the number AAA divisible by 7?
    (A) 1   (B) 3   (C) 6   (D) 7   (E) 9
    answer (D) 7. AAA = 111 × A, and 111 = 3 × 37 shares no factor of 7, so 7 must divide A — only A = 7 works. (777 ÷ 7 = 111. ✓)
